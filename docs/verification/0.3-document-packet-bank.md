## Part I. What exactly is being verified?

### 1. Obligation and object

Document 1 refers to nuclear weapons, nuclear activities, nuclear
technology, and nuclear material.

Identify the immediate object to which safeguards under a Comprehensive
Safeguards Agreement are applied.

Identify the ultimate outcome that the safeguards system seeks to prevent.

Explain why these two objects are related but not interchangeable.

### 2. Structure of the commitment

Reconstruct the safeguards arrangement described in Document 1. Identify:

- the actor undertaking the obligation;
- the conduct required of that actor;
- the object covered by the obligation;
- the verifying institution;
- the proposition that the institution is expected to verify.

For each element, cite the passage on which your answer is based.

### 3. Scope of the conclusion

Consider the following claim:

> "A state under IAEA safeguards has demonstrated that it is not pursuing
> nuclear weapons."

Does Document 1 support this formulation? If not, formulate the strongest
conclusion that the cited provisions do support. Explain what the original
claim adds without textual justification.

## Part III. Testing the analogy

### 8. The Iraq failure

Document 3 describes a regime that could verify declared nuclear material
and inspect declared facilities, yet failed to detect important prohibited
activities.

Explain how both of the following propositions could be true at the same
time:

- routine safeguards were operating at declared facilities;
- the state was developing a clandestine nuclear programme.

Your answer must distinguish the correctness of a declaration from its
completeness.

### 9. Diagnosing the failure

Classify each of the following as principally a legal, technical,
informational, or organisational limitation:

- restrictions on inspector access;
- absence of suitable detection techniques;
- the inspectors' "checklist" approach;
- lack of information identifying locations for investigation.

Some limitations may fit more than one category. Where this is the case,
identify the primary category and justify your choice.

Then explain why simply increasing the number of routine inspections would
not necessarily have solved the problem.

### 10. Access and actionable information

Document 3 states that wider access rights may have limited practical
value without leads concerning where access should be sought.

Explain the distinction between:

- having authority to inspect a location;
- having sufficient information to identify that location as relevant.

Why is this distinction particularly important when the verifier must
establish the absence of undeclared activity?

## Part IV. The disanalysis

### 11. The proposed inference

A policymaker makes the following argument:

> The IAEA verifies nuclear commitments by monitoring specialised physical
> inputs and inspecting relevant facilities. Advanced AI training likewise
> depends on specialised chips. Therefore, a regime based on chip
> registration, logging, and inspection could verify that no prohibited AI
> training occurs.

Evaluate this argument.

Your answer must contain:

- one feature of nuclear verification that genuinely transfers to AI;
- two conditions under which the transfer would be justified;
- one disanalogy, supported by the Iraq case, that defeats the unrestricted
  conclusion;
- an explanation of exactly which inferential step the disanalogy breaks.

Do not merely state that "nuclear weapons and AI are different."

### 12. A limited conclusion

Rewrite the policymaker's conclusion so that it is no broader than the
evidence in Documents 1–3 permits.

Your formulation must specify:

- the category of hardware or activity covered;
- the actors or facilities brought within the regime;
- the kind of violation the regime could detect;
- at least one category of activity about which the regime would not
  justify a conclusion.

Limit: 80 words.

### 13. Repairing the regime

Propose one additional verification layer intended to address the weakness
identified in Question 11. Specify:

- the hidden activity it is intended to detect;
- the information or observable signs it would use;
- the institution that would collect or evaluate that information;
- the event that would trigger further investigation;
- the remaining limitation after your proposal is implemented.

A proposal that claims to eliminate all possibilities of evasion will not
receive full credit.
