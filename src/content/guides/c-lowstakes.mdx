## Session goal

By the end of the meeting, each participant can explain why diffuse failures
need different machinery than concentrated ones, state what sandbagging and
exploration hacking are, and argue one side of the no-sandbagging-on-checkable-tasks
hypothesis. The session assumes participants have watched
[the opening talk](/tracks/control/low-stakes-control/low-stakes-control-talk)
and read
[the non-concentrated failures lesson](/tracks/control/low-stakes-control/handling-non-concentrated-failures).

## Before the session

- Reread
  [the strategic underperformance lesson](/tracks/control/low-stakes-control/misalignment-and-strategic-underperformance)
  and
  [the checkable-tasks lesson](/tracks/control/low-stakes-control/no-sandbagging-on-checkable-tasks) —
  the debate block lives on the second.
- Try [the locking flowcharts exercise](/exercises/c-plm-locking-flowcharts)
  yourself; you will run it as a group.
- If you run a classroom, check the dashboard for who reached
  [the capability-elicitation walkthrough](/tracks/control/low-stakes-control/capability-elicitation-guided);
  seat at least one finisher in each debate team.

## Agenda

### Warm-up: many small actions (10 min)

Ask the room: "What breaks in module 4's machinery when no single action is
catastrophic?" Collect answers on the whiteboard, then check them against the
opening of
[the non-concentrated failures lesson](/tracks/control/low-stakes-control/handling-non-concentrated-failures).

### Naming the failure modes (10 min)

Rapid definitions round: sandbagging, exploration hacking, password-locked
models. A volunteer defines each; the room refines until nobody objects.
Anchor on
[the strategic underperformance lesson](/tracks/control/low-stakes-control/misalignment-and-strategic-underperformance)
and [the password-locked models paper](/tracks/control/low-stakes-control/password-locked-models-paper).

### Flowcharts, together (15 min)

Run [the locking flowcharts exercise](/exercises/c-plm-locking-flowcharts) on
the shared screen, stage by stage. Vote by hand count at each branch before
submitting; the minority explains its branch before you grade.

### The checkable-tasks debate (20 min)

Two teams. One defends the hypothesis in
[the checkable-tasks lesson](/tracks/control/low-stakes-control/no-sandbagging-on-checkable-tasks),
the other attacks it with the strongest counterexample it can build. Five
minutes preparation, four minutes per side, then open floor. Close by asking
which experiments from the module's papers —
[adaptive deployment](/tracks/control/low-stakes-control/adaptive-deployment-paper),
[exploration hacking](/tracks/control/low-stakes-control/exploration-hacking-paper),
[sabotage evaluations](/tracks/control/low-stakes-control/sabotage-evaluations-paper) —
would move the debate.

### Wrap-up (5 min)

Each participant states one way the low-stakes regime changed what they think
monitoring is for. Collect open questions.

## Common sticking points

- Participants map "low stakes" to "low importance". The regime is defined by
  how catastrophe accumulates, not by how much it matters.
- The debate collapses into definitions of "checkable". That is the crux, not
  a distraction — steer the argument onto what checkability requires, and let
  it stay there.
- Elicitation and incrimination blur together. Keep the whiteboard split:
  getting capability out versus proving misbehavior happened.

## After the session

- On the classroom dashboard, check progress into the module's four papers;
  assign each debate team one paper to finish before next session.
- Read written submissions for the module; recurring confusions about
  sandbagging versus low capability are the usual warm-up for the next meeting.
