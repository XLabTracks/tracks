## Session goal

By the end of the meeting, each participant can state the strongest argument
against AI control that they find credible, say how the regime model weighs
control against its alternatives, and defend a budget split with a reason. The
session assumes participants have read at least
[the arguments-against lesson](/tracks/control/how-useful-is-ai-control/how-useful-is-ai-control).

## Before the session

- Work through [the argue-reveal exercise](/exercises/contra-control-argue-reveal)
  yourself so you know both sides of each item before you moderate.
- Open [the regime-states demo](/demos/regime-states) and click through its steps
  once; you will drive it on a shared screen.
- If you run a classroom, check the dashboard for who has reached
  [the allocation capstone](/tracks/control/how-useful-is-ai-control/running-the-model);
  their allocations seed the debate block.

## Agenda

Times are a running clock for a 60-minute session; if a block runs over, take
the time from the regime-model block — the allocation debate is the core of
this session.

### Warm-up: the best case against (0:00–0:10)

Go around the room: each participant names the argument against control they
find strongest, one sentence, no repeats. Tally on the whiteboard, then vote by
hand count for the argument the room finds strongest overall.

Prompts:

- "Which of these arguments would most change what a lab should do this year,
  if it is right?"
- "Which argument would you have found convincing before module 1, but not
  now — and what changed?"
- If answers repeat: "Give me the strongest argument nobody has said yet, even
  if you don't buy it."

### Argue both sides (0:10–0:25)

Run [the argue-reveal exercise](/exercises/contra-control-argue-reveal) on the
shared screen. Per item: one volunteer argues the objection (one minute), one
argues the reply (one minute), then reveal the authored versions and compare
with what the room produced. Close by asking which authored argument was
stronger than the room's version, and why.

Prompts:

- "What did the authored objection include that our volunteer's version
  missed?"
- "Which of these items is a disagreement about facts, and which about
  values?"
- If a volunteer freezes: have them read the objection aloud first, then
  restate it in their own words — restating counts.

### The regime model, together (0:25–0:40)

Step through [the regime-states demo](/demos/regime-states) on the shared
screen, pausing before each step for the room to predict the next one aloud.
Then show [the additive-control demo](/demos/additive-control) and ask the
group what the demo treats as the alternative to buying control. Connect both
demos back to
[the usefulness lesson](/tracks/control/how-useful-is-ai-control/determining-the-usefulness).

Prompts:

- "What would have to change in the safety budget for control to stop being
  worth buying?"
- "What makes a transition between states happen? Name the lever, not the
  date."
- If predictions are all correct: "Which step of this demo would you bet is
  most wrong about the real world?"

### Allocation debate (0:40–0:55)

Groups of two or three agree on a budget allocation as in
[the capstone exercise](/exercises/control-usefulness-allocation); each writes
its split on the whiteboard with a one-line rationale. Each group then
challenges the split it most disagrees with — one exchange per pairing. Close
by locating the splits among
[Plans A through D](/tracks/control/how-useful-is-ai-control/plans-a-b-c-and-d-for-misalignment-risk).

Prompts:

- "What is the marginal dollar in your split buying, concretely?"
- "Which plan does your split assume the world is on — and what evidence would
  tell you it isn't?"
- "What is the smallest change to the situation that would flip your
  allocation?"

### Wrap-up (0:55–1:00)

Each participant states one belief this module moved, and in which direction —
one sentence, no discussion. Collect open questions for next session.

Prompt: "If you had to defend control's usefulness to a skeptical colleague in
two sentences, what would you say now that you couldn't before?"

## Common sticking points

- Groups often treat the arguments against control as all-or-nothing. The
  module's frame is a weighing exercise; push participants to say what the
  argument changes at the margin, not whether control is "refuted".
- The regime model's states read as a timeline. Ask the room what makes a
  transition happen rather than when.
- Allocation debates drift into raw intuition. Require each defended split to
  name the lever it is buying with the marginal dollar.

## After the session

- On the classroom dashboard, check who completed
  [the worked-example lesson](/tracks/control/how-useful-is-ai-control/running-the-model)
  after the meeting; the allocation debate usually pulls stragglers through it.
- Read submitted written responses for recurring misreadings of the
  arguments-against items; open the next session with the most common one.
