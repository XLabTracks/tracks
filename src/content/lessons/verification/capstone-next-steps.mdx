## Five doors into verification work

These are pathway cards, written as if for the page. Names of real people and orgs are placeholders you should check against your own network before publishing — the point here is the shape.

Door 1: Build the mechanisms

For people with, or willing to build, engineering skill: silicon, firmware, embedded systems, cryptography, networking, ML systems.

- The work: tamper-evident enclosures, on-chip attestation, secure boot for accelerators, location verification, network taps, power and EM side-channel sensors, TEE-based confidential inference, zero-knowledge proofs of training properties, memory-wipe and bandwidth-limiting hardware.
- Why it is unusually open: this is a field of roughly a dozen small teams. A competent hardware or crypto engineer who spends six months reading in is close to the frontier. The reading list you already give learners says this plainly — early movers who join soon could have a big impact.
- First move: pick one mechanism, build the smallest possible demo, write it up publicly. The Longview HEMs call and the Cambridge Hardware Assurance syllabus both give you a menu.
- Where it leads: Amodo Design, TamperSec, Lucid Computing, Earendil, Tinfoil, Confidential, EQTY Lab, Ulyssean, OpenMined, or a hardware-security PhD.

Door 2: Analyse the mechanisms

For people who like technical questions but want to write rather than build: feasibility estimates, threat models, cost curves, red-teaming proposals on paper.

- The work: the Verification Research Ideas doc in your own project folder is a perfect specimen — hardware trojan scanning cost estimates, upper-bounding covert communication bandwidth, side-channel inference feasibility. Back-of-the-envelope work informed by expert interviews, aimed at de-risking an assumption so that someone else can justify a large investment.
- Why it matters: negative results are valuable here in a way they rarely are elsewhere. Showing that an approach cannot work redirects money toward the ones that can.
- First move: take one assumption from a published proposal and try to break it in ten pages. Aim for an arXiv preprint or an Oxford AIGI paper rather than a blog post if you want it to travel.
- Where it leads: RAND CAST, IAPS, Oxford Martin AIGI, MIRI Technical Governance Team, GovAI, AVERI.

Door 3: Negotiate and design the agreements

For law, IR, and policy people. The mechanisms are useless without a regime that specifies what is being verified, by whom, at what confidence, with what consequences.

- The work: treaty architecture, inspection protocols, managed access rules, dispute procedures, national implementation, export-control enforcement design, Track II dialogue.
- The transferable-expertise argument: nuclear and chemical weapons verification has eighty years of institutional practice with exactly the problem AI faces — proving a claim about a sensitive facility without revealing what makes it sensitive. VERTIC verified UK warhead dismantlement without exposing weapon design. The IAEA runs a standing inspectorate. The CTBTO operates a 337-station global sensor network with automated classification. Someone who learns that tradecraft properly and then applies it to compute is rare and valuable.
- First move: read one arms-control verification protocol end to end, then write the AI analogue and mark where it breaks.
- Where it leads: LawAI, IAPS, RAND CAST, Simon Institute, CNS Middlebury, VERTIC, NTI, the UK AISI, the EU AI Office, congressional and parliamentary staff roles.

Door 4: Fund, found and organise

For operators, founders and grantmakers. This field is small enough that field-building is a bottleneck rather than an afterthought.

- The work: running programs, standing up new orgs, moving money to hardware R&D, convening hardware-security people who have never thought about treaties with policy people who have never seen a die shot.
- Evidence this is a real gap: two funding rounds have tried to seed this field — the SFF FlexHEG round in 2024 at roughly $4M and Longview's HEMs RFP in 2025 at $2–10M, which explicitly earmarked seed money for a Focused Research Organization that, as of this July, I could not confirm has launched with a founding team.
- First move: pick a gap you can see from where you stand — there is no DC or Brussels equivalent of LISA or Constellation, and there is no dedicated hardware-verification training course anywhere I could find — and run the smallest version of it.
- Where it leads: Longview, Coefficient Giving, Macroscopic Ventures, Foresight, Apart Research, Constellation, or founding something.

Door 5: Translate and teach

For writers, educators and distillers. This track is itself an instance of this door.

- The work: explainers, curricula, distillation of technical work for policy audiences and vice versa, journalism, running reading groups.
- Why it is load-bearing here: verification sits between three communities that do not read each other — hardware security, cryptography, and arms control. Most of the field's failure modes are translation failures.
- First move: distil one technical verification paper for a policy audience and publish it. Then do it four more times.
- Where it leads: BlueDot, Apart, IAPS communications, think-tank comms, independent writing, or back into the track as a facilitator.

Design question: five doors may be one too many for a single page. Doors 4 and 5 could collapse into one "everything else that the field needs" card, or move to a footer. Doors 1, 2 and 3 map cleanly onto the technical / analytic / policy split your learners already self-identify with.
