INF Tech, a Shanghai-based startup, has been remotely accessing about 2,300 leading-edge Nvidia Blackwell chips through an Indonesian data center; as of early 2026 the arrangement is legal under U.S. law (Tan 2026). Three different verification layers could in principle notice this run. Someone tracked those chips when they shipped. Someone could collect intelligence on INF Tech. And one company, the Indonesian provider, already has the whole thing in its billing records. This module is about that third party: the cloud provider.

Frontier models are mostly trained on machines their developers don’t own. A frontier training run needs thousands to tens of thousands of AI accelerators wired together — clusters that live in data centers operated by a handful of companies.

Between the AI developer and the chips sits a landlord. The developer rents; the provider owns, operates, meters, and bills.

## Where cloud sits among the four buckets

Cloud verification is simultaneously the most technically ready and weakest verification bucket. Providers already collect identity data, telemetry, and chip-hours; the KYC (Know Your Customer) mechanism has already been invented. Cloud methods, however, see only actors who choose to rent from a covered provider. Some AI firms run their own data centers; states can incentivize captured providers to obscure the whole evidence base; even perfect KYC coverage pinpoints who the customer is and how much they ran, never what the model was, or for what purpose. Moreover, KYC inherits banking’s failure modes: VPNs can sever accounts from locations, reseller chains degrade visibility, and more. In other words, cloud mechanisms see only actors who stay on covered infrastructure; determined evaders can still fly under the radar.

So why is teaching cloud valuable, if it is a comparatively weak and limited verification mechanism? First, it’s the layer negotiators will realistically face: every U.S. compute governance order has run through providers. Second, it’s immediately implementable: cloud runs on records providers already keep for billing and do not face the delays in building hardware infrastructure or collecting new intelligence. And on a meta-level, cloud exemplifies how technical feasibility is not enough for verification value. It’s still important—for context and comparison—to understand the weaker but historically used and precedented tool.
