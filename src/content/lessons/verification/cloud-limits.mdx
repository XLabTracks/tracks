Every mechanism so far assumes the adversary cooperates by being visible. Drop that assumption, then decide what cloud evidence is still worth.

## The Failure Mode

Suppose the regime works exactly as designed: registrations filed, declarations submitted, thresholds set. A customer files every form—but trains a dangerous model anyway. If compliance is defined as paperwork, the regime measures paperwork.

A 2025 RAND study (Moon et al.) modeled monitoring as a game: the provider moves first, choosing which metrics to watch; the evader moves second, choosing how to train. Providers might not be able to report and detect AI training if obligated to monitor based only on floating-point operation thresholds.

A simple but effective evasion technique to bypass provider monitoring: sequential training, where the training session for a model that would otherwise pass the compute threshold is broken up into a series of below-threshold sessions. Take BLOOM, a real, public 176-billion-parameter model which took about 5.8 × 10²³ operations to train. Against a per-account threshold of 10²³ operations, BLOOM in one piece crosses the line. Split it into 58 sequential sessions, each below the threshold, each handing its weights to the next, across accounts the provider cannot link; and since the provider cannot inspect the weights, it has no way to recognize fifty-eight strangers as one continuing run. It is the $9,900 deposit—with GPUs.

Sequential training evades thresholds by splitting the run in time; distributed training splits it in space. Here the evader runs shards of the same workload simultaneously across several accounts, providers, or jurisdictions, synchronizing between sites, so that no single account accumulates threshold-crossing compute and no single monitor ever holds the whole run in its records. Reassembling the run would take cross-provider information sharing, which mostly does not exist. Frontier labs build low-communication training methods (DiLoCo and its successors) for their own resilience across data centers, and every improvement they publish shrinks the cost an evader pays for violation.

## The Price of Evasion

While providers cannot prohibit evasion, they can make doing so more difficult: every violation of the rules carries costs. A  tripwire that forces evaders into slower, costlier, or more roundabout behavior is both disincentivizing violations in the first place and creating a trail of behavior to track. RAND’s standing recommendation to policymakers is to systematically hunt for detection gaps, then re-hunt as the technology moves. Here are some examples of cost-heavy considerations for customers:

- Cluster size — big models resist scattering: one distributed-training experiment (DiPaCo) needed roughly 32× the compute units of the colocated baseline.
- Data transfers — the dataset has to arrive; a fresh account pulling terabytes in is a tell.
- Instruction mix — training is dense in multiply-accumulate operations, in the regular rhythms of parallel training; diluting the mix costs efficiency.
- Power draw — sustained training burns power at rates little else matches (BLOOM: roughly 3,600 kWh per day).

A core concept to apply anywhere: any fixed line invites slicing; any per-account rule invites more accounts. Identity linkage, KYC’s cross-account job is to turn “58 unrelated accounts” back into “one customer.”

## The Cloud’s Edges

Everything above assumes the compute is rented from a covered provider. Three exits leave the regime entirely:

- Own the machines — build or buy a cluster. No landlord, no records, no switch.
- Rent elsewhere — a provider the regime doesn’t reach. The traffic is real (figures as of May 2026): Alibaba and ByteDance lean on Southeast Asian data centers; Tencent bought access to 15,000 B200 chips through a $1.2 billion deal with a Japanese provider; the Southeast Asian data center market is projected to pass $30 billion by 2030 (Tan 2026).
- Skip the compute — steal the trained weights. That bypass gets its own treatment in 3.1.

Several of the biggest AI developers are their own compute providers, or joined at the hip with one, and when developer and provider are the same entity, the neutral intermediary this module is built on disappears.

Furthermore, implementing evasion protections have two critical costs for providers, which translate into limitations for feasibility: money and privacy.

- Money: Vetting customers and monitoring workloads is expensive for providers, and providers pass the expense on as higher prices and slower onboarding. Customers who have alternatives respond by using them: a foreign lab that can rent comparable chips from an uncovered provider in another jurisdiction will. When it leaves, the regime loses more than a sale — it loses the ability to see that customer at all, because every mechanism in this module only works on customers of covered providers. This is why the strictest rules on paper can produce the least visibility in practice.
- Privacy: Today, providers generally do not inspect what their customers run, and they advertise that fact; confidential workloads are part of what cloud customers buy. A monitoring mandate breaks that promise for everyone — every customer gets watched so that a rare dishonest one might be caught. The design can shrink the intrusion (exempt small users, monitor metadata only, report only on suspicion), but shrink is the most it can do; some surveillance of honest customers is the price of any workload monitoring at all.

There is no version of this regime where that trade disappears.

## Conclusion: What is cloud evidence worth?

You have now seen every mechanism in this module. The skill to take with you is a habit of asking two questions about any piece of cloud evidence before trusting it.

- What does this evidence prove, and what does it merely fit with?
- Who produces this evidence, and what would faking it cost them?

Ask both questions of every record you have met in this module: declarations, KYC files, billing records, utilization curves, logs. The table below runs the exercise for the whole stack.

Yet even at full strength, cloud evidence cannot establish intent (metadata shows a training run, not why), completeness (a clean record at provider A says nothing about provider B, or the basement cluster), or the true principal (KYC names the counterparty; the chain behind it can stay dark). Each missing piece is owned elsewhere: hardware attestation (2.1) for what code actually ran; energy, procurement, and satellite signatures (2.3) for compute nobody declared; insiders (2.4) for intent. One witness, never the whole court.

That fixes cloud’s place among the four layers. It is the most immediately feasible: the records already exist for billing, and every U.S. compute-governance action to date has run through providers. It is also the easiest layer to leave. Hardware mechanisms (2.1) travel with the chips wherever they go; intelligence (2.3) sees actors who never filed anything, at higher cost and lower certainty; human sources (2.4) can reach the one thing no meter shows: intent. Cloud is the layer a treaty can stand up first, but also the layer least able to stand alone.
