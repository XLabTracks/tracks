2.2.1 established what providers can see. This section covers what a treaty asks them to do with it.

## Declaration and Registration

You’ve learned what cloud providers can and cannot theoretically verify about training runs. Now, the question becomes: how can a verification regime operationalize these capabilities into regulation? Regulatory actions for cloud compute can be roughly classified into the two following schemes:

- Registration: A cluster this big exists, here. The provider reports the existence, location, and capacity of frontier-capable infrastructure. A registration threshold is a rate: how fast the cluster could compute, in operations per second.
- Declaration: A training run this big is happening. The customer or provider reports a specific activity above a threshold. A declaration threshold is a total: how much a run consumed, in operations.

Simply put: registration maps where frontier runs could happen; declarations claim which ones are happening.

A useful declaration answers the same three layers as 2.2.1. Who: verified identity and an authorized signatory. How much: hardware, hours, locations. What for: purpose, model type, planned safety evaluations. But even a facially complete declaration is just a claim, and claims can be falsified. Its value is that it can be checked, and that lying in it carries legal consequences. Declared operations have an independent shadow in the 2.2.1 records: chips × hours × utilization.

POP-UP The 2023 US executive order on AI (rescinded in 2025) required reporting of clusters at 10^20 operations per second networked above 100 Gbit/s, and of training runs above 10^26 total operations. For a treaty’s own numbers, Egan and Heim propose pegging the initial threshold to the compute of then-current frontier models and revisiting it as capability per FLOP improves. Any specific exponent is an imperfect and easily-obsolete policy choice—albeit a necessarily concrete one. (See Thresholds Explainer)

CHECK A draft treaty annex sets two thresholds: one in operations per second, one in total operations. Is each threshold a registration or declaration?  (a) The rate triggers cluster registration; the total triggers run declaration. (b) Both trigger declarations. (c) The rate triggers declarations; the total triggers registration. (d) The two are interchangeable.

Answer: (a). A rate describes what infrastructure could do; a total describes what a run did. A regime that confuses them cannot enforce either threshold.

## Know Your Customer: from banks to compute

Export controls stop chip shipments, but they do not stop logins. An entity barred from buying accelerators can rent the same silicon by the hour: “It is not necessary for these entities to own the physical chips if they are accessible digitally” (Egan and Heim 2023).

Every record and declaration is worth little if the account holder isn’t who they say they are. Banking answered this identity verification problem with a strategy called Know-Your-Customer, or KYC—Egan and Heim transfer this model to compute.

What does KYC look like in compute verification?

Above a compute threshold, the provider must:

- Verify the entity: incorporation, registered office, directors, checked against government registries.
- Verify the beneficial owners: the humans who ultimately own or control the customer, behind any corporate wrappers. The US line is 25 percent ownership or substantial control.
- Record a statement of purpose and monitor usage against it, since risk profiles change after onboarding.
- Keep records of above-threshold compute provided, and of denials.
- Screen customers against the US Entity List, sanctions lists, and defined high-risk profiles, and report the matches.
- Investigate sublessors, because compute gets resold and each hop in a reseller chain degrades visibility.

POP-UP  KYC in banking: US banks have run this scheme since the 1970s to prevent financial crime, terrorist financing, and identity theft, based on Bank confidentiality Act and later USA PATRIOT Act foundations, with the global Financial Action Task Force (FATF) setting standards that member states write into domestic law. Compliance stayed weak for years, until penalties got real. It is expensive: US financial-crime compliance cost an estimated 40.7 billion dollars in 2022 (LANDSCAPE), and money laundering persists anyway. Providers will cite this history.

The KYC scheme has two known gaps:

The jurisdictional leak. One jurisdiction cannot hold it alone. A US-only requirement pushes exactly the customers with something to hide toward providers in laxer jurisdictions, selecting for evasion while burdening the honest. Egan and Heim make allied adoption and shared risk information a condition of the design; otherwise the checkpoint becomes a detour sign.

The chain problem. A provider sells capacity to CloudBridge Ltd, a reseller. CloudBridge subleases to Meridian Analytics, a consultancy. Meridian’s compute is used by a client it declines to name. The provider’s KYC file says “CloudBridge”: true and verified, but useless. Treat multi-hop chains as a live gap and as a red-flag pattern to hunt.

CHECK  A customer entity is owned 100 percent by a holding company, which is owned by another company offshore. What does beneficial-ownership verification require? (a) Verifying the holding company’s name and stopping there. (b) Checking that the offshore jurisdiction has a tax treaty. (c) Confirming the customer’s email domain matches its website. (d) Tracing through the layers to the actual humans who own or control the customer.

Answer: (d). Stopping at any wrapper, as in (a), is exactly the failure the requirement exists to prevent.

## Keep, report, suspend

Three duties, in rising order of force:

Keep. Providers retain records of above-threshold compute provided and denied. Denials map who tried and failed; investigations need both sides of the ledger (Egan and Heim). The paper names no retention period, a real design gap.

Report. Routine records mostly stay with the provider; regulators see reports at thresholds, on request, or on high-risk matches. One proposed design is lighter still: providers report only on reasonable grounds to suspect a violation, the way banks flag suspicious transactions rather than mailing every ledger to the government. The trade is visibility against intrusion. More routine reporting means more oversight, and more honest customers’ data in government files.

Suspend. The lever that separates Cloud from every other surface in Module 2: the evidence-holder can also stop the activity. Compare enforcement elsewhere: a chip on the wrong continent is gone. Cloud access is point-in-time, metered, and revocable mid-run, so a violation discovered on Tuesday can lose its compute on Tuesday. Because suspension is fast, precise, and reversible, it is also a lighter trigger to pull, and therefore a more credible threat.

## The Paperwork Regime

A tempting inference: providers must report runs above the threshold, therefore above-threshold runs get caught. Banking named the failure decades ago. The moment a 10,000-dollar reporting line existed, customers began splitting deposits into 9,900-dollar slices. The move has a legal name: structuring. Write a threshold, and you have written the evasion’s spec sheet.

Egan and Heim warn of the compute version: a developer deconstructs a compute-intensive project into smaller, discrete sub-projects that each fall below the reporting threshold (sec. 2.2.2). RAND modeled the general case.

EXCERPT — Moon et al. 2025

“Cloud service providers might not be able to report and detect AI training if they are obligated to monitor and report activity based only on floating-point operation thresholds in future AI governance policy.”

The design lesson: declarations work as commitments to check against, and fail as the only detection layer. A regime built on self-declared forms, with no telemetry corroboration, catches exactly the participants who tell the truth on forms. RAND’s corroborating metrics (data-transfer patterns, cluster size, power consumption) appear in 2.2.3, where structuring comes back with numbers on it.

CHECK  A regime requires declarations but performs no telemetry checks. Which actor does it constrain most?

Answer: Compliant actors. Violators file false forms. That is the paperwork regime.
