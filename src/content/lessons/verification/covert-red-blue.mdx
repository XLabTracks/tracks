### Page 1 of 5: treaty obligations, AI training and chip consolidation

#### Article IV: restrictions on AI training

The proposed agreement separates AI training into three regulatory bands. Runs above a strict compute threshold, including post-training runs above a separate strict post-training threshold, are prohibited. Runs above a lower monitored threshold must be reported before they begin. Runs below that monitored threshold generally may proceed without CTB oversight.

For a monitored run, the party must provide an estimate of total computation and information about the training code and data. CTB staff receive supervised access under confidentiality controls. Written approval from both US and Chinese inspectors located at the training facility is required before the run begins. Inspectors check whether the proposed methods appear on an approved technique whitelist. The CTB may monitor the run, request model checkpoints, and order a pause if concerning capabilities or behavior appear. The agreement also allows approved carveouts for activities judged safe and permits the thresholds and whitelist to be revised.

The design therefore treats compute as a measurable proxy for risk. It does not claim that compute perfectly determines model capability. Verification must establish at least the identity and timing of a run, its cumulative compute, its classification as training or post-training, its approval status, and whether the declared procedure matches what actually occurred.

#### Article V: consolidation and monitoring of AI chips

The agreement defines a covered chip cluster as a connected set above a specified H100-equivalent capacity. Parties must place these clusters in declared, physically accessible facilities and avoid dispersing them across so many locations that monitoring becomes infeasible. Smaller unmonitored holdings may remain outside declared sites only if they are not aggregated, networked, rotated among sites to defeat monitoring, or used for prohibited training.

Within 120 days, each party must locate, inventory, and consolidate its covered clusters. Domestic authorities and the CTB would use site access, records, inspections, accompanied transfers, information sharing, and protected reporting channels. Registers must identify locations, quantities, chip types, available unique identifiers, and interconnects, and must be kept current. Transfers require advance notice, approval, and the possibility of observation. Decommissioned chips remain controlled until their destruction or permanent disablement is certified.

Article V supplies a crucial precondition for workload verification: the relevant chips and facilities must first be found and brought inside the monitored system. A mechanism installed only in declared facilities cannot by itself prove that no undeclared cluster exists.

<Src>Scher et al., Articles IV.1–5 and V.1–7, original PDF-viewer pages 23–26. [An International Agreement to Prevent the Premature Creation of Artificial Superintelligence](https://techgov.intelligence.org/research/an-international-agreement-to-prevent-the-premature-creation-of-artificial-superintelligence), MIRI Technical Governance Team.</Src>

### Page 2 of 5: treaty obligations, chip production and chip-use verification

#### Article VI: monitoring chip production and transfers

The CTB would coordinate monitoring of AI-chip production facilities and important inputs to production. The proposed chain begins with fabrication and extends through high-bandwidth memory and logic production, testing, packaging, assembly, sale, transfer, installation, and eventual placement in a declared cluster. If a facility cannot be monitored and its output tracked, AI-chip production at that facility must stop until acceptable controls are in place.

Transfers of AI chips and manufacturing equipment require CTB authorization and tracking. Transfers among parties receive different presumptions depending on whether the item is a chip or manufacturing equipment. Transfers to nonparties are generally presumed to be denied. The Executive Council could also limit annual production to prevent stockpiles or excess capacity from shortening the time required for a state to break out of the agreement.

These obligations concern the supply chain rather than the internal behavior of a data center. They require facility declarations, production accounting, serialized or equivalent tracking, customs and transfer records, inspections, and legal controls over producers and sellers. Workload evidence from network traffic cannot establish that fabrication, packaging, or an international transfer occurred as declared.

#### Article VII: continuous verification of chip use

Parties accept continuous on-site verification of total chip use at declared covered clusters. The permitted toolkit includes inspectors, tamper-resistant cameras, power and thermal measurements, network characteristics, on-chip or retrofitted controls, workload declarations, and rerunning declared workloads. The purpose is to determine whether chips are being used for prohibited activities, especially training barred by Article IV.

If the CTB cannot obtain sufficient assurance, the hardware must be powered off and its non-operation verified. The CTB may also impose operating restrictions, such as limits on interconnect bandwidth or latency, computation rate, memory bandwidth, or numerical precision, to make prohibited training harder or easier to distinguish from permitted inference. Verification may differ across facilities according to risk and national-security sensitivity, and the CTB is directed to continue research on improved monitoring.

Article VII is the treaty provision most directly connected to the low-trust system in pages 3 to 5 of this packet. That system could contribute workload evidence, commitments, selective challenges, and controlled evaluation. It would still need to operate alongside physical security, independent sensors, shutdown authority, institutional decision rules, and remedies for violations.

<Src>Scher et al., Articles VI.1–7 and VII.1–6, original PDF-viewer pages 29–32.</Src>

### Page 3 of 5: system overview, objectives, properties, and workflow

#### Section 1: purpose and design objectives

Naci Cankaya presents the system as a working draft for privacy-preserving, retrofittable AI-compute verification between rival states. The paper aims to make verification objectives more concrete, propose a high-level reference architecture, and invite technical criticism before laboratory deployment. It is therefore a design proposal and research agenda, not evidence of an already validated system.

The proposed system emphasizes six properties:

- Sensitive user information, training data, model information, and intellectual property should remain inside the monitored facility. Only disclosures agreed in advance should leave it.
- The mechanism should retrofit existing AI hardware using available components rather than depend on a new generation of trusted accelerators.
- Physical security is a prerequisite. Guards, access controls, inspections, and monitoring must establish that the verification devices are installed and connected correctly.
- The parties should not need to trust a single common chip or supply chain. Minimal security-critical components and redundant computation reduce reliance on any one trusted computing base.
- Evidence capture and commitment should be separated from later evaluation. Network taps are the default capture mechanism. Air-gapped auditing environments, or potentially mature zero-knowledge methods, evaluate selected evidence.
- Additional layers, including physical monitoring, redundant recomputation, resource accounting, and memory wiping, should limit evasion and information leakage.

#### High-level workflow

The facility operator is the prover. The party demanding independent evidence is the verifier. Taps and optional sensors capture evidence about workloads executed on the prover's hardware. Because the plaintext may contain secrets, the verifier initially receives only signed cryptographic commitments, such as hashes. The prover keeps the corresponding plaintext locally.

After the records have been committed, the verifier chooses a sample and issues a challenge. The selected plaintext enters a controlled evaluation environment inside the prover's facility. That environment is meant to produce only a pre-agreed compliance disclosure: enough information for the verifier to evaluate the claim, but no unnecessary model, customer, or national-security data. Redundant recomputation and physical monitoring are intended to make manipulation or leakage harder.

The logic depends on commitment occurring before the prover knows which evidence will be challenged. It also depends on captured evidence being complete, retained, attributable to the correct hardware and time, and reconstructable when challenged.

<Src>Cankaya, Section 1 and the opening of Section 2, original PDF-viewer pages 1–5. [A System Overview for Near-Term, Low-Trust AI Compute Verification](https://techgov.intelligence.org/research), MIRI Technical Governance Team.</Src>

### Page 4 of 5: system overview, threat model and practical requirements

#### Section 2: what low trust means

The paper argues that coarse aggregate measures, such as total training compute or the overall division between training and inference, may be insufficient to detect covertly prohibited development or deployment. It therefore proposes collision-resistant evidence linked to individual ML workloads, potentially down to each forward pass. This is an ambitious design choice: it may increase the range of rules that can be checked, but also increases data volume, engineering complexity, and the risk that workload semantics remain ambiguous.

The threat model is deliberately two-sided. A malicious prover may conceal training, run an approved model for a prohibited purpose, suppress records, bypass a tap, or manipulate a declaration. A malicious verifier may use the monitoring system to obtain model weights, code, training data, customer data, or national-security information. Verification succeeds only if it constrains both parties.

The paper models cheating as covert rather than openly hostile. Deterrence depends on the probability of detection multiplied by the cost of being caught exceeding the expected gain. Random sampling can therefore be useful even when every workload is not fully inspected. But the logic becomes weak if anomalies can be dismissed as ordinary malfunction. A failed hash, missing record, or inconsistent rerun does not identify its cause. The parties need testing, known error rates, preserved evidence, and an agreed escalation procedure that distinguishes technical failure from deliberate evasion.

#### Practical requirements

The proposed system should be:

- Retrofittable: it should work with deployed hardware and should not depend on proving that advanced commercial silicon is free of supply-chain compromise.
- Low-friction: cost and performance overhead should be small enough for political and commercial acceptance, and the system should not lower the facility's existing security standard.
- Automated and scalable: manual review should be minimized because the number of screened workloads could be extremely large.
- Flexible: captured evidence should support new compliance rules without replacing the capture hardware.
- Redundant: minimal monitors from different supply chains should cross-check one another; programmable evaluation components require air-gapping and tightly limited disclosure.
- Physically protected: cameras, sensors, inspections, and guards remain necessary because strong anti-tamper assurance against a capable state adversary is unresolved.

These requirements can conflict. Capturing richer evidence helps later analysis but increases cost, privacy exposure, and attack surface. Minimal taps are easier to secure but provide less semantic structure. Stronger inspection improves assurance but may make states or firms less willing to participate.

<Src>Cankaya, Sections 2a–2c, original PDF-viewer pages 5–10.</Src>

### Page 5 of 5: system operation, capture, challenge, and evaluation

#### Section 3: separation of capture and evaluation

The architecture follows a general sequence: capture broadly, commit records immediately, and examine selected samples later. Minimal, non-programmable taps are placed inside the prover's facility. They are intended to parse only limited network structure and output commitments rather than plaintext. Meaningful objects are reconstructed later in the auditing environment.

Because raw traffic does not automatically reveal which bytes correspond to a prompt, completion, model artifact, or other object, the prover may provide a manifest mapping intervals of committed traffic to claimed objects and workloads. The manifest is not trusted as proof. It helps the verifier choose meaningful challenges, while the auditing environment must test the claim against the committed evidence.

#### End-to-end compliance sequence

1. A workload executes on monitored servers.
2. Its relevant communications cross a tapped and sanitized network path.
3. The tap creates signed commitments while the prover stores the plaintext evidence locally.
4. The verifier selects a random or risk-based sample after commitment.
5. The challenged plaintext is reconstructed inside a secure local auditing environment.
6. Programmable analysis, and where feasible re-execution, tests whether the workload matches the declaration and applicable rule.
7. The environment releases only a pre-agreed finding or limited disclosure.
8. Redundant components cross-check the capture and evaluation results.

The design favors retrospective screening: records can be revisited with improved analysis methods, making it harder for an evader to rely permanently on a weakness in today's detector. Yet this benefit depends on the continued availability and integrity of stored evidence.

#### Assumptions and limits that remain open

The execution trace assumes the system has already been installed securely. It also assumes that a monitored pod cannot communicate except through the tapped, sanitized front-end path. High-bandwidth back-end compute and storage fabrics are harder and more expensive to tap, so incomplete coverage is a central risk. Other unresolved issues include reproducible re-execution, hidden state, misleading manifests, side channels, compromise of inspectors or hardware, false-positive attribution, evaluation at massive scale, and whether a compliance finding will lead to a timely institutional response.

For the treaty comparison, the most important scope distinction is this: the system may help explain workloads inside a known monitored facility, especially for Articles IV and VII. It does not by itself locate chips, prove inventory completeness, monitor fabrication or international transfers, decide sanctions, or enforce shutdowns. Those Article V and VI functions require separate technical and institutional mechanisms.

<Src>Cankaya, Sections 3.1–3.2, original PDF-viewer pages 10–12.</Src>

## Section B — post-case taxonomy revisited

You have now tried to construct and detect an evasion scheme. Return to the taxonomy and add judgment. The scores below are relative, conditional assessments—not measured probabilities. They depend on the agreement, actor, hardware fleet, jurisdiction, access rights, and verification architecture.

Scoring: 1 = very low or weak; 2 = low; 3 = moderate; 4 = high; 5 = very high. For verification effectiveness, a higher score is better for the verifier. “Module priority” reflects emphasis in this course, not a claim about numerical likelihood.

### Repurpose declared infrastructure — module priority 4/5

| Technical feasibility for evader | Political / organizational feasibility | Verification effectiveness | Durability / harm |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **4/5 — High.** The hardware, staff, and site already exist. | **4/5 — High.** A small number of authorized insiders may be enough. | **3/5 — Moderate.** Logs and telemetry help, but purpose and intent are difficult to infer. | **5/5 — Very high.** Standing capacity can support repeated activity. |

Often the most realistic route for an actor that already controls major compute. Legitimate activity provides cover: training can be labeled as inference, or prohibited capability work as safety or maintenance. Broad workload telemetry can produce useful signals, but proving the exact research purpose requires clear rules plus access to raw scheduler records, code, data, checkpoints, and personnel evidence. [14][21]

*Who can change the balance:* Developers; cloud providers; national regulators; inspectors; hardware and standards bodies

### Steal or copy model weights — module priority 3/5

| Technical feasibility for evader | Political / organizational feasibility | Verification effectiveness | Durability / harm |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **3/5 — Moderate.** Requires insider access or a serious cyber compromise. | **3/5 — Moderate.** Can be done by a small team, but access and confidentiality are demanding. | **2/5 — Low.** There may be no new large training signal. | **5/5 — Very high.** One successful copy can be duplicated and reused. |

Weight theft changes the verification problem: the actor may acquire a powerful model without recreating its original training run. Access logs, segmentation, data-loss controls, and human reporting can help, but attribution and proof of what was copied are difficult. Compression research may reduce exfiltration time, but exfiltration remains a multistep attack. A compute-only agreement does not close this route. [16][17]

*Who can change the balance:* Frontier developers; cloud providers; security teams; national regulators; law enforcement

### Split training across sites — module priority 3/5

| Technical feasibility for evader | Political / organizational feasibility | Verification effectiveness | Durability / harm |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **2/5 — Low today.** Low-communication methods are improving, but frontier-scale cases remain extrapolative. | **2/5 — Low.** Many sites, operators, and coordination points increase exposure. | **2/5 — Low.** Single-site thresholds and local power signals may miss the whole operation. | **3/5 — Moderate.** Repeatable in principle, but inefficient and operationally fragile. |

Recent work makes it unsafe to assume that all frontier training must occur inside one tightly connected facility. However, the largest governance-relevant scenarios are modeled rather than directly demonstrated. Distribution reduces the value of site-level thresholds while increasing procurement, accounting, staffing, networking, and whistleblower surfaces. Treat this as an emerging and uncertain route, not a solved capability. [15]

*Who can change the balance:* Treaty designers; chip and cloud regulators; intelligence agencies; inspectors; hardware owners

### Falsify declarations or provenance — module priority 3/5

| Technical feasibility for evader | Political / organizational feasibility | Verification effectiveness | Durability / harm |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **5/5 — Very high.** Documents and curated logs are easy to alter when the actor controls them. | **4/5 — High.** Requires control of records and cooperation or silence from relevant staff. | **3/5 — Moderate.** Independent records and cross-checks can expose contradictions. | **3/5 — Moderate.** One false record may cover one run; a captured system can conceal repeated activity. |

False reporting is usually a supporting tactic rather than a complete development strategy. It becomes less effective when records are generated automatically, preserved outside the actor’s control, and compared with procurement, power, cloud, chip, code, checkpoint, and human evidence. Verification must test both whether declared activity occurred as claimed and whether important activity was omitted. [14]

*Who can change the balance:* Cloud and hardware providers; developers; auditors; inspectors; regulators

### Distill or extract capability — module priority 3/5

| Technical feasibility for evader | Political / organizational feasibility | Verification effectiveness | Durability / harm |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **3/5 — Moderate.** Depends on model access, query budget, data, student compute, and output richness. | **4/5 — High.** May require no insider and can use proxy accounts or intermediaries. | **3/5 — Moderate.** Providers can monitor access, but defenses are emerging and threat-model dependent. | **3/5 — Moderate.** A student model can spread, but may not reproduce all teacher capabilities. |

Distillation is not the same as weight theft. It trains a new model to imitate behaviors exposed through an interface; it does not recover the teacher’s exact parameters. Providers can use identity checks, rate limits, anomaly detection, output design, fingerprinting, or watermarking, but recent research finds that defense performance depends heavily on attacker assumptions and can trade off against service quality. [18]

*Who can change the balance:* API providers; model developers; regulators; identity and payment providers

### Conceal the real actor — module priority 2/5

| Technical feasibility for evader | Political / organizational feasibility | Verification effectiveness | Durability / harm |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **5/5 — Very high.** Shell companies, nominees, and borrowed accounts are cheap to create. | **4/5 — High.** Cross-border finance and ownership chains require coordination but are well established. | **3/5 — Moderate.** Ownership, payment, address, communications, and logistics links can expose networks. | **4/5 — High.** A proxy network can support repeated purchases and replace exposed entities. |

Proxy organizations are a high-feasibility enabling tactic, not a substitute for chips, power, engineering, or a viable workload. The A.Q. Khan network demonstrates how distributed intermediaries and front companies can route around supplier controls and exploit jurisdictional seams. Beneficial-ownership and reseller-chain analysis should therefore examine linked entities, not each nominal customer in isolation. [3][4][5]

*Who can change the balance:* Cloud and compute providers; exporters; financial institutions; corporate registries; regulators

### Divert controlled hardware — module priority 2/5

| Technical feasibility for evader | Political / organizational feasibility | Verification effectiveness | Durability / harm |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **3/5 — Moderate.** False documents and transshipment are feasible; covert frontier-scale accumulation is harder. | **3/5 — Moderate.** Requires brokers, logistics, financing, and cross-border concealment. | **3/5 — Moderate.** Customs, finance, supplier records, serials, and inspections create leads. | **4/5 — High.** Individual shipments are episodic, but acquired hardware and networks can persist. |

Recent U.S. cases allege front companies, false paperwork, dummy servers, and transshipment through third countries to divert controlled AI hardware. These cases show realistic tactics, not proven prevalence; charging documents are allegations unless and until established in court. Acquiring chips is only one step: the actor must still power, cool, network, maintain, and use them. [19]

*Who can change the balance:* Vendors; distributors; customs; export-control agencies; freight and financial institutions
