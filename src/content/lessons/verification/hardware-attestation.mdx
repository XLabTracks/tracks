
### 2.1 Hardware

<Objectives scope="the core section">
1. **Bound a hardware claim.** Distinguish what a mechanism identifies, attests, measures, classifies, restricts, or reconstructs, then state what remains outside the claim.
2. **Trace the trust and authority chain.** Name who measures, signs, defines acceptable state, updates, revokes, appraises, decides, and enforces.
3. **Invert the threat model.** Reassess the same mechanism when the operator is a cloud customer, a laboratory with physical control, or a state-backed owner.
4. **Compare trust architectures.** Evaluate on-chip, off-chip, and hybrid designs for the same policy claim.
5. **Recommend a bounded role.** State what hardware should carry in a layered regime, what it should not carry, which independent evidence must corroborate it, and what new evidence would change your recommendation.
</Objectives>

#### Inside the Chip

Somewhere on the die of a modern AI accelerator, a small block of logic does
something the rest of the chip cannot: it holds a secret. The secret is a
private key, either burned into one-time-programmable fuses on the production
line or derived from physical variation in the silicon itself, and the
circuitry around it is built so the key can be used and not read out.
Everything this section calls attestation stands on that arrangement. A signed
report is a statement the chip makes with a key nobody else is supposed to
have.

Two facts about that key decide most of what follows. It was provisioned in a
factory, by the manufacturer, before anyone knew which laboratory or which
country the chip would work in. And the reference values a verifier compares
the chip's report against are published by that same manufacturer. The chip
can testify. It testifies in a language the vendor defined, with a key the
vendor gave it, about the facts the vendor decided were worth measuring.

Against most adversaries that is a strong arrangement. For a treaty it is a
strange one, because the party holding the chip is the party under suspicion,
and the key in the chip does not answer to the verifier.

#### The Central Question

A signed statement can be authentic and still be too narrow, stale, incomplete, or based on a compromised measurement. Hardware becomes useful for verification only when the whole chain is specified: the rule, the measurement, the evidence, the trust assumptions, the decision authority, and the response.

Module 1 established why compute is the input a regime can see at all:
detectable, excludable, quantifiable, and made in a concentrated supply chain
([Sastry, Heim, Belfield, et al., 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.08797)).
This section asks a different question. Not whether compute is governable in
principle, but what a particular chip can testify to, to whom, and with what
left over.

#### What This Section Teaches, and Where

Hardware proposals serve several different functions. They are not a ladder
from weak to strong: a mechanism can do one of these well and contribute
nothing to another.

| Function | The verifier's question | Taught in |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Identify | Which device is this, and is it covered? | 2.1.2, 2.1.3 |
| Attest | What hardware, firmware and security state is present now? | 2.1.2 |
| Locate | Where is the device, and how is it connected? | 2.1.3 |
| Measure | How much counted compute occurred? | 2.1.4 |
| Classify | Was the workload training, inference, or something else? | 2.1.4 |
| Restrict | Was the activity permitted, and who can stop it? | 2.1.5 |
| Reconstruct | Did the declared process produce the submitted model? | Optional: 2.1.7 |

Where the trust for all of this should sit is 2.1.6, and what role hardware
should be given in a layered regime is 2.1.8.
