Whistleblowers report when they choose or when a duty requires it. Audits and inspections let a verifier look on a defined schedule, at a defined trigger, or in response to a specific concern.

## Three mechanisms, three purposes

| Mechanism | Typical trigger | Main purpose | Main design risk |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Independent audit | Periodic, milestone-based, or pre-deployment review | A third party tests specified claims and evaluates systems or practices against a standard. | The auditee controls the auditor, scope, evidence, or publication. |
| Routine inspection | A schedule, random selection, or recurring agreement obligation | Confirm declared sites, inventories, records, processes, or controls. | Predictability permits staging, and undeclared activity may sit outside the routine route. |
| Challenge inspection | A specific concern, anomaly, or formal request, usually on shorter notice | Clarify possible noncompliance that routine measures did not resolve. | Delay, political misuse, excessive intrusion, or access negotiated below what the concern requires. |

These mechanisms collect and assess evidence. They do not necessarily make the legal decision. A sound process distinguishes:

1. **Collection:** what the team obtained, observed, preserved, and could not access.
2. **Assessment:** what those facts support, under stated assumptions and uncertainty.
3. **Adjudication and response:** whether the facts satisfy the agreement’s standard and what an authorized body should do.

## Access sets the ceiling on the conclusion

[Brundage et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.11699) define frontier AI auditing around “deep, secure access to non-public information.” Their assurance levels make a simple point: as the auditor relies less on the company’s own representations and sees more of the organization, the audit can support broader and more confident claims.

- **Black-box access:** the auditor sends inputs and observes outputs. It can support a claim about model behavior under the tested conditions. It cannot establish training history, compute use, internal deployment, or executive decision-making.
- **Gray-box access:** the auditor also receives selected internals, tools, records, or privileged interfaces. This can test more explanations, but the conclusion remains bounded by what was selected and what remains hidden.
- **Deep access:** the auditor can examine relevant model internals, training processes, compute allocation, incident and governance records, and staff interviews. It can test organization-level and historical claims if records are complete and the auditor has authority to follow emerging concerns.

The paper’s higher assurance levels add duration and resistance to deception: from a time-bounded system audit, through months of documentation and cross-functional interviews, toward ongoing oversight and continuous verification. The highest levels remain an aspiration, not a label an auditor should claim because the engagement was expensive or technically sophisticated.

An audit conclusion is valid only for the systems, entities, evidence, claims, and period actually examined. Findings should state their assumptions and expire or be revisited when a release, incident, configuration change, or other event makes them stale.

## What the mandate must settle

A usable audit or inspection mandate answers these questions before an access dispute begins:

- **Who may inspect?** Name the body, required expertise, independence and conflict rules, security requirements, and any process for objecting to an individual inspector.
- **What is in scope?** Specify models, code, evaluations, compute records, governance decisions, facilities, affiliates, cloud regions, colocation sites, and contractor-controlled systems.
- **When may access occur?** Define routine timing, random selection, challenge triggers, notice periods, duration, and event-triggered review.
- **What may the team do?** Cover document review, system access, testing, observation, sampling, copying, log export, reconciliation, and private staff interviews.
- **Who must cooperate and preserve evidence?** Extend duties to relevant developers, affiliates, cloud and data-center operators, suppliers, and contractors; prohibit alteration or deletion after notice.
- **How is sensitive information protected?** Set rules for vetted personnel, secure rooms, read-only systems, redaction, aggregation, limited retention, purpose limitation, and sanctions for misuse.
- **What follows delay or refusal?** Define deadlines, alternative-access procedures, escalation, reporting consequences, and any authorized adverse inference, penalty, suspension, or referral.

Refusal can establish a breach of an access obligation. It does not automatically prove the concealed activity. Whether a decision-maker may draw an adverse inference depends on the agreement.

## Managed access

Access need not mean copying every trade secret into a regulator’s general files. [Wasil et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2408.16074) treat inspections as access-dependent but note that limited access can protect privacy and proprietary information while still answering a verification question.

For an AI inspection, managed access might allow a vetted subset of the team to inspect model weights on site, provide a read-only query over raw scheduler logs, disclose a cryptographic commitment instead of source material, shroud unrelated equipment, or return an independently generated aggregate. The inspected party may propose an alternative, but the alternative must answer the same question. A company-produced summary is not a substitute for raw allocation evidence when the question is whether an undeclared above-threshold training run occurred.

The practical test is narrow: does the arrangement let the verifier reach a defensible conclusion while withholding information that is genuinely unrelated to that conclusion?
