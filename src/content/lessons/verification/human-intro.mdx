Technical monitoring can show that a cluster drew power, that chips moved, or that a model produced a particular output. It may not show why the work was authorized, whether a safety warning was suppressed, or whether an organization prepared a false account for an inspector. Those are human questions.

Human sources can expose an undeclared site, explain a project code, identify the people behind a decision, or point an investigator to records that would otherwise remain hidden. They can also be mistaken, deceptive, pressured, or unable to report safely. The useful question is therefore not whether a source seems trustworthy in the abstract. It is what the source could actually know, which parts of the account remain inference, and what independent evidence could test the material claims.

Three research programs supply the structure for this section:

- [Wasil et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2408.16074) treat whistleblowers and on-site inspections as distinct verification methods. Their practical point is that “no single method is foolproof”: reporting, records, physical access, and technical monitoring cover different failure modes.
- [Baker et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2507.15916) place whistleblowing, personnel interviews, and intelligence in a personnel-based layer. That layer is comparatively easy to deploy, but its strength depends on how many people know about a violation, whether they can coordinate a cover story, and whether any of them can contact a verifier.
- [Brundage et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.11699) describe frontier AI auditing as third-party assessment based on “deep, secure access to non-public information.” Their account connects staff interviews to model internals, compute records, governance decisions, and safeguards for sensitive information.

Together, the papers support a limited but important role for human evidence. A source often tells the verifier where to look and what to preserve. Records, inspections, and technical signals then test the report. An audit can combine those streams, but only within the scope and access its mandate permits.

This section proceeds in four parts:

- **2.4.1** maps which people across the AI supply chain can observe which activity, and where their knowledge stops.
- **2.4.2** examines reporting channels, whistleblower protection, and the path from an allegation to usable evidence.
- **2.4.3** compares audits and inspections, including how access conditions limit the conclusion.
- **2.4.4** asks whether the verifying institution is independent and capable, and when the evidence supports investigation, a compliance finding, or enforcement.
