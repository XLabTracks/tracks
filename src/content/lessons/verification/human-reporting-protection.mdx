A reporting system has two jobs. It must give people a route they can realistically use, and it must turn what they submit into evidence without exposing the source unnecessarily or overstating the claim.

An engineer may risk employment, unvested equity, immigration status, litigation, or future work by reporting a safety override. A contractor may have weaker legal protection and no internal channel. A technically secure form is not enough if it can be traced through a work device, access log, document history, or the small number of people who knew the fact.

[Wasil et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2408.16074) propose a practical package: protected and anonymous channels, legal and job support, incentives for verified information, and cross-border protection. They also warn that incentives do not help if an organization can block contact with the verifier. Regular private contact with personnel can provide a second route.

## What a usable channel requires

- **Internal and external routes.** A reporter should be able to contact an independent internal office, a regulator or treaty verifier, and—where the law permits—another competent authority. Requiring internal reporting first can expose the source or give implicated managers time to remove evidence.
- **A real choice between anonymity and confidentiality.** Anonymous reporting withholds identity from the recipient. Confidential reporting reveals it to a restricted group. The channel should state the limits of both arrangements.
- **Secure two-way contact.** Investigators need a way to ask follow-up questions and give preservation instructions without forcing the reporter to disclose more identifying information than necessary.
- **Routing around conflicts.** A report about senior management must not pass through senior management for approval.
- **Clear triage.** The recipient should distinguish an urgent safety or evidence-destruction risk from a report that can enter the ordinary investigation queue.

## AI whistleblowing: SB 53 and the broader design test

California’s SB 53 created a specific statutory floor for AI whistleblowing, now codified in Labor Code §§1107–1107.2. Its definition is deliberately tied to safety work:

> “Covered employee” means an employee responsible for assessing, managing, or addressing risk of critical safety incidents. — [California Labor Code §1107](https://www.leginfo.legislature.ca.gov/faces/codes_displayText.xhtml?article=&chapter=5.1.&division=2.&lawCode=LAB&part=3.&title=)

The law bars a frontier developer from making or enforcing a rule, policy, or contract that prevents such an employee from making a protected disclosure, or retaliating for it. The employee must have reasonable cause to believe the information shows a specific and substantial public danger from a catastrophic risk or a violation of the covered frontier-AI statute. Protected recipients include the California Attorney General, a federal authority, and specified people inside the company with power to investigate or correct the problem. Large frontier developers must also provide a reasonable anonymous internal process, send the reporter monthly status updates, and share reports with officers and directors while excluding a leader accused of wrongdoing.

This is not a rule that every NDA is void or that every AI concern is protected. It is a rule against using contracts to block disclosures that fall within the statute. The distinction matters when a verifier tells a source what protection actually exists.

The [AIWI/CARMA Best Practice Guide](https://aiwi.org/ai-whistleblowing-law-best-practice/) states the broader principle directly:

> “Whistleblower rights must override confidentiality/nondisclosure agreements.”

The guide would extend protection beyond SB 53’s covered employees and catastrophic-risk pathway. Its recommendations include duty speech by evaluators and safety staff; contractors, former employees, board members, and third-party assessment organizations; confidential and anonymous external reporting; remedies that account for equity and visa-related retaliation; and a receiving agency with the expertise and authority to act. These are not details around the edge. They determine who can report, what they can report, and whether a nominal right can be exercised before retaliation makes it useless.

## Four ways to make reporting more viable

| Mechanism | What it changes | Main limitation |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Anti-retaliation protection | Reduces the expected personal cost and provides a remedy for firing, demotion, threats, blacklisting, or lost compensation. | A right may arrive too late unless temporary relief and practical legal support are available. |
| Financial reward | Offsets personal cost and can motivate disclosure of useful original information. | It creates an incentive to examine, not a reason to accept or reject the claim. Corroboration still controls weight. |
| Mandatory reporting | Makes silence a breach for specified roles or organizations. | It produces paperwork rather than verification if the duty is vague, unenforced, or sent to an office that cannot act. |
| Professional or organizational duty | Makes escalation part of the role of safety staff, auditors, security personnel, executives, or boards. | The duty collapses when management rewards a clean record more than early discovery of problems. |

The mechanisms can be combined. They should not be treated as substitutes for safe channels or competent investigation.

## From report to evidence

1. **Receive and scope.** Record when and how the report arrived, its claimed urgency, the reporter’s identity preference, and the material allegation.
2. **Route and preserve.** Bypass implicated people. Retain the original submission, attachments, and available metadata; assign a case identifier; and record access, copying, and transfer.
3. **Authenticate.** Test whether the source plausibly held the claimed role and whether submitted records are what they appear to be. Authentication establishes provenance, not truth.
4. **Investigate.** Seek records, technical signals, physical observations, and sources the reporter does not control. Record contradictions and plausible alternative explanations as well as support.
5. **Separate fact from inference.** Keep the source’s observations, investigator findings, and analytical conclusions distinct.
6. **Transmit a scoped package.** Send the authorized verifier the allegation, source-access assessment, preservation record, corroboration, contradictions, unresolved questions, handling restrictions, and recommended next step. Share identity only when necessary and authorized.
