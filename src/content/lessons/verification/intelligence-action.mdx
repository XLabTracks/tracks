A lead has arrived and been assessed. This section is about what the regime
does next, and about doing it proportionately, which is the part that decides
whether a verification regime survives its first ambiguous case.

## Comparing Against Declarations

The first move is the cheapest: set the observed activity against what was
declared, and ask for clarification. Most anomalies die here, which is the
point. A regime that escalates every anomaly has no capacity left for the ones
that matter.

## Escalating

When clarification does not resolve it, the ladder runs roughly:

- **Task additional collection**: look again, with a different stream.
- **Seek corroboration**: find an independent source that sees the same thing.
- **Trigger an audit or challenge inspection**: the treaty's own tools, which
  is where identification hands off to resolution.

Each rung costs more, politically and diplomatically, than the one below.
Thresholds are priced by both error costs: a false alarm spends regime
credibility, a miss spends the treaty.

The ladder tops out at a *regime* action by design. A kinetic strike is not a
rung on it and not a verification finding: it can level a facility but cannot
adjudicate whether a violation occurred. Resolution stays with regime
adjudication; the clean worked case is the Iran record, where an intelligence
tip opened the file and IAEA complementary access resolved it.

## Recording What You Did Not Resolve

The step that gets skipped. Whatever the chosen action, the record should carry:

- The **confidence** attached to the assessment;
- Any **dissent** among the analysts;
- The **unresolved blind spots**: what nobody could see, as distinct from what
  was looked at and found clean.

An assessment that hides its own uncertainty is worth less than one that states
it, because the next decision-maker cannot calibrate on it. Dissent is data.
This is the same discipline as the evidence standards in the capstone.

## The Analyst Desk

The full run: six signals, worked one at a time under the ladder's discipline.
At most two collection moves per signal, then a disposition committed with
confidence, dissent, and a blind spot on the record. The debrief prices each
call; the report at the end reads your pattern, not just your score.

Taught as live controversies, not settled facts: each is a place where
serious people disagree, and you should be able to hold both sides.

The course is self-paced, so a debate is not a room; it is a short two-sided
memo. Pick one controversy and hold the rest as reading. Steelman both poles
in turn, a short paragraph each, written to satisfy that pole's strongest
advocate. Then commit to a position in a sentence or two and name the
evidence that would move you off it. Holding both sides is the point, not
winning. If you want a live opponent, spar one pole against an AI tutor
arguing the other, and have it press on your weakest link before you write
the commit.

**1. Is intelligence decisive evidence, or only a lead?** The cautionary
pole: the 2002 to 2003 Iraq WMD estimate, where the US intelligence
community was "dead wrong" in almost every judgment
([the Robb–Silberman report](https://irp.fas.org/offdocs/wmd_report.pdf)):
high-confidence claims resting on a single fabricator (Curveball), misread
aluminium tubes, and forged Niger documents, all presented to the UN as
decisive. The counter-pole
([Baker](https://arxiv.org/abs/2304.04123),
[*Six Layers*](https://arxiv.org/abs/2507.15916)): intelligence as lead
generation and tripwire still works. In the IAEA record, tips identified the
undeclared facilities and the regime resolved them. The module's resolution
is the identify/resolve split: the failure mode is treating a lead as a
verdict, not using leads at all. The discipline's own corollary: analysis is
about "portraying uncertainty," not manufacturing certainty
([Lowenthal](https://collegepublishing.sagepub.com/products/intelligence-10-288967)).

**2. How much verification is enough: "adequate" or "effective"?**
Carter-era doctrine required adequate verification (detect militarily
significant cheating in time to respond); the Reagan administration raised
the bar to effective verification, which Paul Nitze operationalized as "high
confidence, not perfect confidence"
([CRS R41201](https://www.everycrsreport.com/reports/R41201.html)). The
deeper point
([Krass](https://www.sipri.org/sites/default/files/files/books/SIPRI85Krass/SIPRI85Krass03.pdf)):
there is no objective technical answer to how high a detection probability
must be. The threshold is a political judgment, and no treaty is ever fully
verifiable. The provocation at the far end: sometimes the verification
burden is not worth it
([Kimball, "Trust, but Don't Verify"](https://www.armscontrol.org/act/2004-09/issue-briefs/trust-dont-verify)).
For an AI pause you must pick a defensible confidence target and own its
false-positive and false-negative tradeoff.

**3. NTM sufficiency versus the necessity of intrusive access.**
[National technical means](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_technical_means_of_verification)
(satellites, SIGINT) were sufficient to count large fixed objects, silos and
launchers, and treaty law protected them with noninterference clauses. But
the IAEA story shows the limit: comprehensive safeguards plus NTM could
verify declared material yet were structurally blind to undeclared activity
(Iraq, North Korea), which is exactly why the intrusive
[Additional Protocol](https://www.armscontrol.org/factsheets/iaea-safeguards-agreements-glance)
had to be invented. Verifying correctness of a declaration is easy;
verifying completeness, that nothing undeclared exists, is the hard problem,
and it is the AI-pause problem.

**4. Commercial-satellite transparency: democratizing boon or spoofable
liability?** The boon: commercial Earth observation has exploded, many firms
now sell frequent sub-5-metre optical and radar imagery, the IAEA runs a
[Satellite Imagery Analysis Unit](https://www.iaea.org/publications/magazines/bulletin/57-2/completing-the-picture-using-satellite-imagery-to-enhance-iaea-safeguards-capabilities),
and NGOs do
[independent verification](https://media.nti.org/pdfs/The_Opportunities_and_Limits_of_Societal_Verification.pdf).
The double edge: imagery has
[hard physical limits](https://nonproliferation.org/op-44-eyes-on-u-opportunities-challenges-and-limits-of-remote-sensing-for-monitoring-uranium-mining-and-milling/)
(uranium mines look like copper mines, in-situ leaching leaves almost no
footprint, thermal readings throw false positives); "open" imagery is not
neutral, since firms
[withhold under government request](https://www.timesofisrael.com/satellite-firm-says-its-indefinitely-withholding-iran-war-images-at-us-request/)
(shutter control); and AI-generated satellite imagery threatens the
evidentiary value of the pictures themselves.

**5. Sources-and-methods versus sharing, and the credibility problem.**
Sharing a tip with a rival or an international body risks burning the
collection that produced it, so arms-control treaties impose no duty to
share; sharing is voluntary and inconsistent
([Baker](https://arxiv.org/abs/2304.04123)). And shared evidence "might be
fake": the Niger forgeries are the standing warning. For a US–China pause
the design question is how to make a tip credible to Beijing without
exposing how you know, the bridge to the confidentiality module's
cryptographic tools (a pointer only, per the boundary contract).

**6. Meta-debate: is the nuclear analogy the right frame at all?** Arms
control is the richest verification precedent we have, and this module leans
on it. But a serious critique holds the nuclear frame is
[misleading for AI](https://ai-frontiers.org/articles/nuclear-non-proliferation-is-the-wrong-framework-for-ai-governance):
weights are not fissile material, there is no bulky unavoidable input to
meter (chips are the closest), diffusion is faster, and dual-use is total. A
[systematic review](https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.21203) of how the analogy is
used across the field catalogs these objections while arguing analogies can
still inform policy despite the domain differences. The module's stance:
reason with analogies as scaffolds, never as proofs.
