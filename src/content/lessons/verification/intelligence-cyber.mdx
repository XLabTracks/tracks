Interception of communications and electronic emissions, and collection from
networks and systems themselves: who is talking to whom, and what a facility
is actually running, read from the inside without anyone's permission. The
AI-verification literature never separates the two — Scher and Thiergart give
them one row, *Six Layers* lists them together, and the MIRI draft's
definition of national technical means names both — so this section follows
the literature and takes them together.

## What the Literature Says

*Six Layers* names signals and cyber intelligence, with human intelligence, as
the disciplines of its national-intelligence layer, in the paragraph quoted at
the head of this module — effective without the Prover's cooperation — and
says no more about either. Scher and Thiergart fold them into one row and rate
the pair High feasibility within a year:

<SourceQuote
  t="Mechanisms to Verify International Agreements About AI Development"
  url="https://arxiv.org/abs/2506.15867"
  what="the locating-compute appendix (pp. 67–80) — the ‘cyber operations and communication surveillance’ row"
  by="Scher and Thiergart | MIRI (2024)"
>

Signals-based intelligence is already in use. For detecting AI compute, such
approaches might focus on AI developers, data centers, and chip producers.

</SourceQuote>

That is the whole of it. The MIRI draft agreement goes one step further and
writes both words into its definition of national technical means: Definition
17 counts "satellite, aerial, cyber, signals, imagery (including thermal), and
other remote-sensing capabilities employed by Parties for verification
consistent with this Agreement." None of the precedent definitions the draft
borrows from — the ABM Treaty, INF, CTBT, New START — named cyber.

## What the Precedent Carries

The precedent is richer than the AI literature: in arms control, national
technical means — satellites and signals intelligence together — were
sufficient to count large fixed objects, silos and launchers, and treaty law
protected them with noninterference clauses. What that record does and does
not carry over to a training run is one of the debates kept for 2.3.11.

## The Characteristic Limits

Two, and the second sharpens the first.

Signals intelligence is the most sources-and-methods-sensitive stream, which
is exactly why it is the hardest to share with a treaty verifier: showing what
was heard shows how it was heard. Nuclear treaties impose no obligation on
intelligence agencies to share with treaty verifiers, and sharing is voluntary,
informal, and inconsistent (Baker §2.3.3) — protection of sources and methods
is *why*. Cyber collection carries the same limit with more force: evidence
collected from inside a rival's networks is the least shareable evidence there
is, because showing what was found shows how it was reached. Carry that into
the memo: a stream that cannot be shown to the other side can raise a lead,
and cannot by itself settle one.

The inclusion of cyber is the contested part. Read from one capital, "cyber"
inside NTM licenses intrusion as treaty-protected verification; read from the
other, it is a licensed intrusion program aimed at itself, and the word the
other side tries to strike first. The collection asymmetry between the parties
is what makes the same clause read as protection to one and threat to the
other. A cyber-derived lead can open a file; what resolves it is the regime's
own tools, which is the identify-and-resolve split of this module's opening
applied to its sharpest case.
