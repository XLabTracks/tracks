The previous sections were about what a watcher can see and conclude. This one
is about the institutions that do the watching, and what a treaty can say about
them.

The precedent is asymmetric, and the asymmetry is the lesson: **weak on
sharing, strong on protection.**

## Sharing

Nuclear treaties impose no obligation on intelligence agencies to share with
treaty verifiers. Sharing is voluntary, informal, and inconsistent (Baker
§2.3.3) — and protection of sources and methods is *why*. Third-party verifiers
lack major states' collection capabilities, so the body formally charged with
verifying is rarely the body that can see.

## Protection

Noninterference-with-NTM and no-deliberate-concealment clauses are standard
arms-control furniture: no blinding satellites, no nets over mobile missiles.
Rose Gottemoeller, the US chief negotiator for New START, called NTM
noninterference "one of the earliest and easiest points of agreement" in those
negotiations.

This is the low-hanging fruit of the whole module. Note the contrast with
sharing, where drafting is hard — a point worth carrying into the mark-up
below.

## Architecture: National or International?

*Six Layers* argues — in an emphasis footnote, so hold the modality — for
relying on **national** agencies rather than empowering an international agency
to spy. Three reasons: decades of built-up capability; the IAEA precedent of
learning about violations from national agencies; and the new risks an
espionage-authorized international body would itself create.

The consequence is that weak-intelligence states free-ride on stronger states'
collection. In the US–China case, each side trusts its own agencies to verify
the other.

Read against the current US hard-sovereignty posture (NSS 2025), NTM is the
*sovereignty-compatible* form of verification — which is why a Washington
allergic to intrusive inspectorates could still sign the NTM article. The
accountability cost rides along: evidence only some parties can see, unequal
collection capacity, and no one to verify the verifier's claims.

## Authenticating What Gets Shared

Baker's "shared documents might be fake" problem is an evidence-credibility
question, and it belongs here: how do rivals authenticate intelligence passed
between them — signal authentication, chain of custody?

Kissinger states the general form: rules need common understanding of
capabilities, "but these are precisely the capabilities the major actors will
be reluctant to disclose" (*World Order*, pp. 199–201).

The cryptographic machinery for this — zero-knowledge proofs, multi-party
computation, attestation — stays in 2.0. The bridge worth knowing is the
zero-knowledge warhead-verification precedent (Glaser, Barak and Goldston,
*Nature*, 2014).

## A Tip Is Only Worth What the Receiver Can Check

That table is why the sharing clause stays voluntary — and it sets up the
problem your red-line meets next: **a tip is only worth what the receiving
side can check.** Al-Kibar, 2007: the package that crossed the alliance table
reportedly held photographs from inside the reactor building — evidence the
recipient could test against its own imagery archive of the same site.
Authentication rode on the recipient's independent collection, not on trust.

Turquzabad, 2018–19: a rival's seized archive pointed at specific locations.
The IAEA never adjudicated the archive's authenticity as a document trove — it
went to the named sites, took environmental samples, and let the uranium
particles speak. The pattern generalizes: **named coordinates beat narrative
claims, and physics beats testimony.** A shared tip is credible in exactly the
degree it is independently checkable.

The design consequence for an AI regime: you cannot draft the free-riding
away, and you cannot mandate sharing — but you can build the regime so tips
arrive *checkable*: declarations to compare against, access rights to invoke,
an analytic bench of its own. How much of that your NTM article says out loud
is a choice you make at the mark-up below.

## Concrete Text

MIRI's draft agreement adopts the arms-control boilerplate wholesale.
Definition 17 defines national technical means to include satellite, aerial,
cyber, signals, and thermal-imagery remote sensing, with noninterference and
no-concealment language borrowed from the ABM Treaty, INF, CTBT, and New START.

<Fold label="After you commit: the annotated exemplar">

A model NTM article, annotated: where the sharing clause breaks, and how each
of the five criteria scores. Open this only after your own answers are committed —
score yourself against it, honestly. It is one defensible answer, not *the*
answer.

**Def. 17: "…satellite, aerial, cyber, signals, imagery (including
thermal)…"** — *Strike "cyber," or qualify it to passive collection.* "Cyber"
inside NTM is the quiet landmine: it licenses intrusion as treaty-protected
verification. The precedent NTM definitions never named it. Washington wants
it in; Beijing reads it as a licensed intrusion program aimed at itself — this
is the word the other capital tries to strike first.

**Def. 17: "…employed by Parties…"** — *Amend to "employed by or on behalf of
a Party," with one line on commercially tasked collection.* Whose satellites
count? Commercial constellations under national tasking, allied services, NGO
imagery — "employed by" does silent work, and shutter-control practice shows
commercial imagery is already an instrument of state policy. Left vague, the
definition invites a dispute at the first challenge.

**Article (b): "…consistent with generally recognized principles of
international law."** — *Keep as is.* The ABM-lineage hedge. It imports the
unresolved argument over what espionage international law permits — and that
is fine: precedent fidelity beats novel drafting on a clause that has signed
five treaties.

**Sub-clause i (noninterference).** — *Keep verbatim.* The easy signature —
Gottemoeller's "earliest and easiest point of agreement." Any "improvement"
here spends negotiating capital on the one clause that costs nothing.

**Sub-clause ii (no deliberate concealment).** — *Add an illustrative,
non-exhaustive list: masking of thermal, power, or construction observables at
covered facilities.* The AI-specific pressure point: is behind-the-meter
generation "deliberate concealment" or ordinary industrial practice? Without
an illustrative list the clause is unenforceable against exactly the pattern
that matters (the Memphis workaround).

**Sharing — the article says nothing.** — *Keep the silence, then build beside
it: a voluntary tips channel with authentication and chain-of-custody handling
(the 2.0 machinery), explicit sources-and-methods protection, and no duty to
share.* The discovery the task is built around: a sharing *duty* is where
drafting dies — no arms-control treaty has ever imposed one, because a
mandated tip burns the collection that produced it. The honest article names
the dependency on voluntary tips instead of pretending to solve it.

**Why Washington signs and stays — model paragraph.** Washington signs because
the article legalizes what it already does best. The NSS 2025 posture is hard
sovereignty: no intrusive inspectorate on US soil, no obligations that
constrain freedom of action — and NTM verification is the one form that asks
nothing of it, while its collection advantage does the treaty's detection
work. Noninterference protects sensors it already flies; no-concealment binds
the party it watches more than it binds itself. It stays as long as the treaty
never obliges it to reveal what it knows or how it knows it — which this
article, deliberately, never does.

**Why Beijing signs and stays — model paragraph.** Beijing can sign the same
article for its own reasons. Noninterference is sovereignty-compatible —
Deng's "national sovereignty is far more important than human rights"
generalizes to any regime that keeps foreign inspectors off Chinese soil — and
a seat at the drafting table answers the standing grievance of rules made
without China (*World Order*, p. 133). The honest caveats: Kissinger's China
chapters describe an order-conception that is not simply Westphalian, so
treating sovereignty-noninterference as shared common ground is an
approximation, not a fact; and the collection asymmetry — US capacity dwarfs
China's — is why "cyber" in Def. 17 is the word Beijing strikes first. The one
PRC-authored primary in the packet points the same way: the Global AI
Governance Initiative's own language — "respect other countries' national
sovereignty," oppose "intervening in other countries' internal affairs" — is a
sovereignty-first AI posture on the record, compatible with an NTM article
that keeps inspectors out and satellites lawful. It is a diplomatic
initiative, not a treaty position, so it anchors tone, not terms.

Score yourself against the five criteria:

- **Correctness against the signature catalog** — the signatures appear where
  the clauses need them: thermal and power observables in the concealment
  list, the Memphis pattern as the enforcement test.
- **Precedent fidelity** — noninterference kept verbatim; edits confined to
  the definition and the concealment list, where precedent is silent.
- **Honesty about sharing** — no duty proposed; a voluntary authenticated
  channel plus a named dependency. If your draft "solved" sharing with an
  obligation, this criterion is where it fails.
- **Both-capitals plausibility** — each paragraph argues from that capital's
  stated posture and supplied supports; the Beijing paragraph carries its own
  uncertainty flags rather than insider confidence (mirror-imaging check).
- **Blind spots** — military facilities (covered by 2.4 and the hardware
  layer), software-level violations (2.2), and the eroding energy signature
  (3.1's scenarios) are named, each with its sibling owner.
