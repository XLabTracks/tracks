
Let’s talk about what a frontier training run physically is, because the physics is what makes this whole layer possible.

Somewhere there is a building. Inside it are tens of thousands of accelerator chips, and every one of them came from somewhere: fabricated at one of the handful of leading-edge fabs you mapped in Module 1, shipped across borders, bought with purchase orders, installed by contractors. The building draws something on the order of a hundred megawatts, the consumption of a small city, continuously, for months. Every watt of that electricity ends up as heat, because that is what computation does to energy, so the facility has to dump city-scale heat into the air or a river, day and night, all winter. The money involved runs to billions once you count the chips, the construction, and the power contracts, and money moves through banks and contracts that leave records. And several hundred engineers had to be hired, which means job postings, moved equity, and LinkedIn.

A state can lie in its declarations. It cannot opt out of thermodynamics, customs, payroll, or the electrical grid. Each of those is a place where a secret program touches the world other people can see, and some of the touches are visible from orbit. Intelligence, as this course uses the word, means collecting and reading those traces without the cooperation of the actor being watched.

That independence is what earns this layer its place next to hardware as one of the two the track weights most. The mechanisms in 2.1 mostly need chips designed or retrofitted for the job, and the ones in 2.2 see whatever passes through a covered provider. Intelligence is how you watch the actor who signed nothing, declared nothing, or is lying, and it requires no buildout: the satellites are already flying, the customs data already accumulates, the analysts are already at their desks. If an emergency pause started tomorrow, this layer would be the one working by evening.

The real problem here is ambiguity. A hundred-megawatt building full of accelerators might be a covert training site, or it might be a video-streaming company’s new region, and from the outside they can look identical. Several hundred data centers in the world are large enough to be candidates. So the questions that organize this section are the ones a working analyst faces in order: which traces are worth watching, and what does each one actually establish, taken one collection discipline at a time (2.3.1–2.3.5)? Who can see a program from inside it, and what makes them talk (2.3.6–2.3.7)? What does granted access add that watching cannot (2.3.8–2.3.9)? How do you read ambiguous traces without fooling yourself, which turns out to be the hardest part of the job (2.3.10)? And when you have the picture, what do you tell the person who has to act on it (2.3.11)?

The goal is that by the end you should be able to take signals, whether they’re thermal plumes, an import spike, a suspicious hiring wave, and say three things about it: what it establishes, what else would explain it, and what it justifies doing next.

## Intelligence-Based Mechanisms

The mechanisms in 2.1 and 2.2 assume cooperation: hardware roots of trust
need chips built or retrofitted for the purpose, and provider reporting covers
only what passes through a covered provider. Intelligence-based mechanisms do
not. The question for this module is how states can identify possible
undeclared frontier-AI development without the monitored actor's cooperation,
and what a watcher can conclude from what it sees.

The layer has two properties: it works without the monitored actor's
permission, and it needs no new hardware or treaty machinery. The three papers
this module draws on name it differently. Wasil et al. call it the national
technical means (NTM) category, *Six Layers* calls it Layer 6, and Scher and
Thiergart call it the national-intelligence building blocks.

<SourceQuote
  t="Verification methods for international AI agreements"
  url="https://arxiv.org/abs/2408.16074"
  what="p. 1, the abstract — the three-category definition"
  by="Wasil, Reed, Miller, and Barnett (2024)"
>

We divide the verification methods into three categories: (a) national
technical means (methods requiring minimal or no access from suspected
non-compliant nations), (b) access-dependent methods (methods that require
approval from the nation suspected of unauthorized activities), and (c)
hardware-dependent methods (methods that require rules around advanced
hardware).

</SourceQuote>

NTM is defined by what it does not need: access. That is why it is the
fallback when a suspected state refuses to cooperate. Wasil's NTM list is
remote sensing, whistleblowers, energy monitoring, customs-data analysis, and
financial intelligence. Human sources are part of the category from the start,
which is why the human layer is covered in this module, as 2.3.6–2.3.9, rather
than as a module of its own.

*Six Layers* places national intelligence activities (human, signals, and
cyber intelligence) among its personnel-based layers, as mechanisms that may
be effective without the Prover's cooperation, and notes that states with weak
intelligence capabilities could rely on stronger states'. The passage is
reproduced in full in 2.3.6, with the rest of §4.3.

*Six Layers* classes the technical streams of this module (satellite imagery,
open sources, and financial audits) as "merely supplemental": limited in scope
or circumventable on their own. It adds that an attempt to circumvent them
tends to trigger the other layers. Its example: hiding a datacenter underwater
means contracting one of the few firms that can build one, which exposes the
program to those firms' whistleblowers and to intelligence.

The precedent: of the four states the IAEA investigated for undeclared
facilities (the cases in the track's nuclear-precedents section), three
investigations began mainly or entirely with national-intelligence tips and
none through the agency's own safeguards; Baker concludes that the tips
"appear to be irreplaceable"
([Baker, pp. 14–15](https://doi.org/10.48550/arXiv.2304.04123)). Baker also
records the limit: intelligence evidence is characteristically suggestive
rather than decisive or internationally credible, because satellite images
are ambiguous and shared documents may be forged. A regime therefore treats a
tip as the start of a process, not as a verdict.

The distinction that runs through the module: intelligence identifies, the
regime resolves. A tip is not a finding.

## Signatures

Each signature in this module is described in the same three parts: what it
tells a verifier, what access it requires, and its main caveat. One question
applies to every signature: would it survive an adversary who knows it is
being watched?

Signatures fall into four families:

- **Facility signatures** — what the building is and looks like.
- **Resource-flow signatures** — power, water, chips, money moving in.
- **Organizational signatures** — who is hired, who contracts with whom.
- **Operational signatures** — what the site does, and when.

Provider-reported utilization belongs to 2.2, the declared side. This layer
covers what never enters a report.
