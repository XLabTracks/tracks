Permits, filings, hiring, supplier disclosures, published interconnect and
power-purchase records: everything a program leaves in the open. It is the
cheapest stream to collect and, in *Six Layers*' terms, clearly circumventable
on its own — which is why that paper classes open-source intelligence with the
supplemental mechanisms, and why this is the stream a program sanitizes first.

## What the Literature Gives It

*Six Layers* carries the fullest treatment the core literature gives open
sources, and it is one entry long:

<SourceQuote
  t="Verifying International Agreements on AI: Six Layers of Verification for Rules on Large-Scale AI Development and Deployment"
  url="https://arxiv.org/abs/2507.15916"
  what="§4.4, the supplementary verification mechanisms — the OSINT entry"
  by="Baker, Kulp, Marks, Brundage, and Heim | RAND (2025)"
>

Open-source intelligence (OSINT): Open-source information, such as social
media posts and news reports, could reveal information such as hidden data
center constructions, especially in combination with inspections of suspect
sites (“Inspections for undeclared AI clusters” above). Open-source
information could also find signs of non-compliant AI deployment, e.g.,
through its economic, scientific, or military impacts. However, these impacts
might not be evident before the deployment causes harm or yields an unfair
advantage.

</SourceQuote>

Two jobs, then, and they are different: finding a build while it is being
built, and noticing a deployment by its effects — the second of which, the
paper says itself, may arrive late. The same paper gives the stream a third
job in passing, in a footnote on how a verifier checks who a Prover's
employees really are: publication records and social media are how you
confirm that the people put forward for interviews exist and know what their
roles require. Open sources check the human layer too — a bridge to 2.3.6.

The MIRI draft agreement puts open-source intelligence into the pipeline by
which chips would be located — beside supply-chain tracking, mandatory
reporting, state intelligence, power monitoring, challenge inspections, and
whistleblowers (*The Agreement*; Appendix D for the method) — and, in its list
of what can be done before any agreement exists, makes it a recommendation:

<SourceQuote
  t="An International Agreement to Prevent the Premature Creation of Artificial Superintelligence"
  url="https://arxiv.org/abs/2511.10783"
  what="What can we do today? — the recommended measures"
  by="Scher, Abecassis, Barnett, and Abeyta | MIRI (2025)"
>

Invest in verification expertise and know-how, for instance by funding pilot
verification efforts using open-source intelligence or satellite data.

</SourceQuote>

The other two core papers give open sources no entry of their own. Wasil et
al.'s nearest method is customs-data analysis — import and export records read
for unexplained movements of critical hardware, equipment, or raw materials,
which are official records rather than open ones — and Scher and Thiergart's
locating-compute table has no OSINT row. When you write the memo, that is a
fact about the field, not a gap in your reading.

## The Stream in Practice

What the papers describe in a paragraph, a handful of public trackers now do
for real, and the imagery card below is one of them. The power explainer in

intelligence: commercial imagery and permits, cooling equipment read off the
roof, then modelled power and compute.
