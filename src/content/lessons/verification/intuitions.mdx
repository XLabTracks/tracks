Now that we’ve established the foundational motivations for why verification is important, we’ll now dive into building key intuitions around the timelines, scope, and urgency of what a verification regime might actually look like. By the end of this section, you will be able to:

- Describe the general directions the AI arms race could take, and the policy posture that produces each one.
- Explain several distinct ways a verification regime can fail, and trace each failure to its cause.
- Reason about the tension between transparency and confidentiality: monitoring intrusive enough to catch cheating can expose secrets worth stealing, while monitoring loose enough to protect secrets leaves room to cheat covertly.
- Hold rough timelines for the arrival of dangerously capable AI, and use them to think about urgency and prioritization.

What is the ideal end state? What agreement reaches it? What would verification have to cover for the agreement to hold? The most detailed public attempt to answer all three is AI 2040: Plan A, published by the AI Futures Project, the team behind the earlier AI 2027 scenario. While AI 2027 dramatized how a race ends badly, Plan A tells a dated, concrete story in which a US–China deal, layered verification, and a managed slowdown deliver a good outcome by 2040. Its verification supplement ([ai-2040.com/supplements/verification-plan](https://ai-2040.com/supplements/verification-plan)) specifies the machinery: mutual compute declarations checked by inspections, datacenters retrofitted so that large-scale training is detectable, optical network taps feeding trusted recomputation servers, secure R&D facilities, and production caps on unverified hardware.

Below, you will read and gauge the feasibility and robustness of Plan A yourself. Read with a critical but thoughtful eye. For every date, percentage, and recommendation, ask yourself: what assumptions have to be true in order for this to hold? What existing or historical precedents have pointed to this mechanism succeeding or failing? What alternative solutions or estimates could there be, and how does this hold up against them? What is left uncertain? Remember: there is no correct answer, but you want to back your opinions with logic and evidence.

## Commitment enforcement needs verification — curated readings

The simulation built intuition and the scenarios exercised imagination. These three readings supply the evidence base, and the vocabulary the rest of the track uses. Suggested approach: skim all three, then deep-read the one closest to your interests.

- Nuclear Arms Control Verification and Lessons for AI Treaties (Baker, 2023, arXiv:2304.04123). The deepest case study of the one regime that made rival superpowers’ commitments checkable. Read for which nuclear techniques transfer to AI and which do not, and why the differences (compute moves, software copies, capabilities hide in weights) are exactly where AI verification gets hard.
- Verification for International AI Governance (Oxford Martin AI Governance Initiative, 2025). A map of the verification approaches now on the table and the political conditions each requires. Read for the framework connecting technical mechanisms to the agreements they could support.
- Mechanisms to Verify International Agreements About AI Development (Scher and Thiergart, MIRI Technical Governance Team, 2025, arXiv:2506.15867). The source of this track’s working definition: verification mechanisms are processes or tools that give one party greater confidence that another is following agreed rules, typically by detecting violations. Read for the mechanism catalog, and for its accounting of which mechanisms exist today versus which still require R&D.

Keep your Plan A stress-test nearby while you read. These papers are the raw material for judging plans like Plan A, and by the end of the track you will use them to write assessments of your own.
