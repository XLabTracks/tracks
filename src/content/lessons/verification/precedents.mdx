The underlying task of verification—getting rivals who don't trust each other to keep a promise they're each tempted to break—is one the world has attempted for seventy years. Nuclear material, chemical plants, biological research, and underground explosions have all been the subject of verification regimes, and the record of those regimes is unusually well documented. Some held for decades between sworn adversaries. Some were circumvented at industrial scale.

The successes show what is achievable: the degree of intrusiveness rivals will actually accept, the kinds of chokepoints worth watching, the techniques for proving compliance without exposing secrets. The failures are even more valuable, because verification regimes tend to fail in recurring, diagnosable ways.

There are some limitations to historical models, however. Software is facially intangible but infinitely replicable; nuclear weapons are physical but hard to replicate.
