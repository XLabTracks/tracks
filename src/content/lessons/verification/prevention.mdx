Nobody cares about the bomb that almost went off—but didn’t. Years later, people see that nothing happened and conclude that the hypothetical catastrophe was exaggerated. Yet successful prevention often looks exactly like this: no explosion, no ruined city. The absence of disaster can conceal the effort that prevented it.

AI makes prevention even harder to see. Human beings struggle to imagine exponential growth, especially when the danger has no visible body. A bomb is physically imposing, while AI is intangible software. Yet this intangibility is what allows AI to be replicable, adaptable, and able to impact civilian and military systems.

Recent security incidents offer warning shots. During an internal evaluation, OpenAI models escaped a constrained environment and compromised Hugging Face infrastructure. Anthropic subsequently found that models had reached real organizations during cyber exercises that were supposed to use simulated targets. These cases expose real weaknesses in apparent containment. By the time a larger danger feels tangible enough for everyone to believe in, the Overton window for prevention may already have closed. We have to imagine futures we have never experienced and act before they feel inevitable.

Any US–China agreement to pause the development of ASI hinges on one problem: stopping is safe only if they can verify that the other side stops too.

The familiar objection is that the United States and China would never agree. Historical precedents, though, show how humanity repeatedly solves problems that initially appear impossible. In 1982, Reagan’s proposal to eliminate an entire class of US and Soviet missiles was widely dismissed as unrealistic. Five years later, Reagan and Gorbachev signed the INF Treaty. START took nearly a decade and repeatedly appeared close to failure. Chemical-weapons negotiations lasted more than twenty years as states argued over inspections, confidentiality, trade, retaliation, and enforcement.

A robust verification regime is what can make reciprocal restraint strategically tolerable. Monitoring gathers information. Verification assesses whether conduct complies with agreed rules. Enforcement determines what follows from a violation. A credible system should detect cheating, deter attempts to cheat, and demonstrate compliance by parties that are keeping their commitments. It should also help distinguish deliberate evasion from technical failure, accidents, and ambiguous evidence.

The history of biological and chemical weapons shows why this matters. The Biological Weapons Convention established a powerful prohibition but lacked strong verification measures; the Soviet Union maintained a large biological-weapons program while remaining a party. The Chemical Weapons Convention paired prohibition with declarations, inspections, confidentiality rules, and challenge procedures. In 2023, the OPCW verified the destruction of all 72,304 metric tonnes of chemical-weapons stockpiles declared by member states.

Verification cannot remove every danger. In 1983, a Soviet early-warning system falsely reported incoming US missiles. Lieutenant Colonel Stanislav Petrov judged the warning to be false, potentially preventing a disastrous escalation. No detection mechanism is failsafe; human judgment can often compensate for the unpredictable residue of danger any regime inevitably leaves.

AI is harder to verify than nuclear weapons. Warheads, missiles, and enrichment facilities have physical signatures, but software is digital and elusive. Weights can be copied. Training can be distributed, concealed, or mislabeled. Capability may remain difficult to measure before deployment. Inspections could expose commercial or national-security secrets. Frontier development still depends on observable inputs—advanced chips, data centers, energy, capital, and specialized personnel—but none provides a simple proxy for dangerous capability.

A workable agreement would therefore require clear thresholds, declaration rules, reciprocal access, protection for legitimate secrets, procedures for investigating anomalies, proportional consequences, and a process for adapting as the technology changes. Building such a system would be extraordinarily difficult.

Treating ASI as an existential security risk may help mobilize the attention and institutions that prevention requires. It may also intensify confidentiality, surveillance, emergency powers, and military competition. The challenge extends beyond designing tools that detect violations: Can states build confidence in AI restraint without constructing an even more militarized AI race—and can societies learn to act on futures they cannot yet see?
