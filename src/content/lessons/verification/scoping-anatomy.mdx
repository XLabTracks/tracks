## How an agreement is put together

Before you open the treaty, know what you are looking at.

**A treaty has standard furniture.** A title, a preamble, then the main body — definitions, scope, the obligations themselves, the institutions that run them — then the final clauses, and often annexes. The final clauses are the part nobody reads and everybody needs: dispute settlement, entry into force, provisional application, denunciation and withdrawal.

**Most of a treaty binds nobody.** Obligations belong in the operative articles or the annexes, not in the preamble. A preamble carries the reasons the parties give for acting; afterwards its job is interpretation, not obligation.

**You tell the two apart by grammar, not by subject.** A preambulatory clause opens with a participle and ends in a comma — *Alarmed by…*, *Affirming…*, *Recalling…* An operative clause has a subject and a finite verb, and it does something — *Each Party does not develop…*, *The coalition shall implement…* The heading above a clause is not evidence of which kind it is, and you are about to meet one that proves it.

**The method is rarely in the article that creates the duty.** Technical detail goes into an annex, or is left to a body to set later. When you go looking for how compliance would actually be checked, expect to find it somewhere other than where the rule is stated.

**The final clauses decide whether the agreement exists at all.** Entry into force says when it starts and for whom; withdrawal says how a party leaves and on what notice. A verification regime that begins only on entry into force may never begin — the Comprehensive Nuclear-Test-Ban Treaty has been open for signature since 1996, is still not in force, and its monitoring system has been running the whole time.
