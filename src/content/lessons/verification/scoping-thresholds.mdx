# 1.0.1 Thresholds: compute vs. capability

A pause treaty needs a trigger — a measurable line that determines which training runs are covered. Two candidate metrics dominate: how much compute goes into training a model, and what the model is capable of. This section only draws the distinction; the deeper treatment of capability measurement lives in the Evals module link.

#### Compute thresholds (input metric)

The line is drawn at total training compute, in FLOP. Examples in current policy: the EU AI Act's 10²⁵ FLOP systemic-risk presumption and the 10²⁶ FLOP reporting threshold in the now-rescinded US EO 14110.

Pros

- Objective and black-and-white — a FLOP count is a number: an enforceable threshold without ambiguity (GovAI).
- Measurable early and externally verifiable — you can check it before and during a training run, not just after, which is what treaty enforcement requires.
- Correlates with capability and risk — imperfectly, but usefully.
Cons

- It's a proxy, and proxies can be gamed. Algorithmic efficiency improvements mean models trained below the line keep getting more capable — a fixed FLOP number silently loosens over time, and developers can deliberately optimize under it (Hooker; Institute for Law & AI).
  - Regulators partly compensate by making thresholds adjustable (the EU Commission can amend its figure by delegated act).pl

#### Capability thresholds (output metric)

The line is drawn at what the model can do — e.g. "provides meaningful bioweapons uplift." In principle this is a closer heuristic to the dangers we actually care about.

Pros

- Measures danger more directly than compute by targeting the specific qualitative harms models may enact
Cons

- Evals are currently unreliable and qualitatively ambiguous — sensitive to prompting and elicitation effort, plagued by contamination, with no consensus on what score means "dangerous" (Can We Trust AI Benchmarks?).
- Measured too late — capabilities are assessed after training, when the thing a pause is meant to prevent already exists.
- Hard to verify externally — a contestable measurement makes a weak treaty trigger (Oxford AIGI survey).

#### Why this track uses compute

Capability measures the dangers from superintelligence we actually care about, and the trouble is that we're bad at measuring it. Evaluations are unreliable, results shift with prompting and scaffolding, and every judgment call is a seam a rival can dispute. “Our model passed our test" is a claim you're asked to take on faith.

Compute is only a proxy. No particular number of arithmetic operations is dangerous by itself. But compute is physical. It runs on chips from a handful of factories, draws megawatts of power, and lives in buildings visible from orbit. You can count it, and, more importantly, your rival can count it without trusting your word.

So: compute measures roughly the wrong thing precisely; capability measures the right thing badly. Because a treaty trigger must be verifiable and enforceable between parties who don't trust each other, this course, like most current policy, uses a black-and-white FLOP threshold as the operative line.

This is an old move, not an AI compromise. In 1963 the Limited Test Ban Treaty banned nuclear tests in the atmosphere, in space, and underwater, but left underground tests alone. The reason was pure verification: atmospheric fallout could be detected worldwide without anyone's permission, while underground tests could not be told apart from earthquakes at the time. The treaty covered exactly what national technical means could see and permitted what they could not. Compute is our atmospheric fallout: not the thing we fear, but the thing a rival can measure without trusting our word.

The FLOP threshold has a known weakness we'll return to in Module 3: better algorithms let an actor stay honestly under the threshold and still reach dangerous capability, so any fixed number erodes over time.
