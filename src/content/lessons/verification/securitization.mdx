Security scholars did not build the concept of securitization to flatter it. They built it as an instrument of suspicion. In the Copenhagen School's account, developed by Buzan, Wæver, and de Wilde in the 1990s, an actor securitizes an issue by casting it as an existential threat to something a community values, and that framing, once an audience accepts it, unlocks powers that ordinary politics would refuse. The concept earns its keep by forcing a skeptical question. Who gains when a danger gets labeled existential, and what do they hope to move through while the public is afraid?

That suspicion is well founded. States have repeatedly inflated a threat to authorize what they already intended to do. The sharpest modern example followed the attacks of 2001, when the vocabulary of existential emergency licensed mass surveillance, indefinite detention without trial, and an invasion of Iraq justified by claims of weapons that inspectors never found. The pattern recurs with grim reliability. An actor names a catastrophe, declares the usual constraints suspended, and acts before opposition can organize. Securitization theory trains students to distrust that maneuver, and the historical record vindicates the training.

We apply the concept with that record in full view, and we still reach a firm conclusion. Advanced artificial intelligence belongs to the narrow class of threats where the existential framing describes reality rather than exploits it.

What separates a real case from an opportunistic one is evidence and structure, not the volume of the alarm. Consider who is raising it. The laboratories with the strongest commercial reason to dismiss the danger instead document it, and the policies now under discussion would constrain those same laboratories first, from independent evaluation to transparency to verification. When the parties best positioned to profit from denial choose to warn, their warning earns a different presumption than the ones scholars taught you to distrust.

The parallels to earlier catastrophic technologies run deep, and they are structural rather than rhetorical. Advanced AI is dual-use, it carries irreversible downside, it rewards whoever races hardest, and it puts a hard verification problem at the center of any agreement. Those are the precise features that produced the nuclear, biological, and chemical regimes. A capable system threatens nation states with strategic surprise, threatens firms with risks they cannot contain, and threatens the public with harms that outrun any remedy. We govern the older three hazards with treaties, inspections, and export controls. The same reasoning extends to this one.

One distinction answers the critique head on. The abuse of securitization always takes the shape of a blank check, the claim that any measure becomes justified the moment catastrophe is invoked. We ask for nothing so open ended. We argue for scrutiny, for the serious examination of policy, and for the specific class of measures that arms control has already tested, each one calibrated to what the evidence will bear. Section 0.2 begins that work, turning the argument into the concrete task of deciding what to weigh and how heavily.
