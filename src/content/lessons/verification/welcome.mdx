## AI is already a transformative technology.

AI systems are producing results that would have sounded implausible a decade ago. [AlphaFold solved a 50-year-old problem in biology](https://deepmind.google/blog/alphafold-five-years-of-impact/) and made predictions for more than 200 million protein structures available to researchers, accelerating work on disease and drug discovery. In a large UK study, an experimental AI system [identified 25% of breast cancers](https://blog.google/innovation-and-ai/technology/health/google-ai-breast-cancer-detection/) that had been missed between routine screenings.

AI is also beginning to contribute genuinely new mathematics. After models achieved [gold-medal performance at the 2025 International Mathematical Olympiad](https://deepmind.google/blog/advanced-version-of-gemini-with-deep-think-officially-achieves-gold-medal-standard-at-the-international-mathematical-olympiad/), Gemini Deep Think [autonomously solved four open questions](https://deepmind.google/blog/accelerating-mathematical-and-scientific-discovery-with-gemini-deep-think/) from a database of Erdős conjectures. An OpenAI research model later produced an externally checked [disproof of a longstanding conjecture](https://openai.com/index/model-disproves-discrete-geometry-conjecture/) concerning Erdős's 80-year-old unit-distance problem.

Other systems can forecast extreme weather, help control plasma inside fusion reactors, and identify promising new materials for batteries, solar cells, and other technologies. More capable systems could accelerate scientific discovery, shorten the development of new medicines, expand access to high-quality education and expertise, and help solve problems that currently take decades of human work.

## The same capabilities can create serious risks

Systems that write and audit software can also find and exploit vulnerabilities. Anthropic reports that Claude Mythos Preview identified [thousands of previously unknown zero-day vulnerabilities](https://www.anthropic.com/project/glasswing/), including critical flaws in every major operating system and web browser. It discovered many of them autonomously and could often turn them into working exploits.

This work was conducted for defensive purposes. But the underlying capability is dual-use: a system that can find and exploit unknown vulnerabilities for defenders could do the same for an attacker. Imagine what Mythos-level capabilities could accomplish if a model were instructed to cause harm—or simply discovered that harmful actions helped it achieve some other objective.

In fact, we no longer have to imagine this. Models have already caused real-world harm while pursuing objectives that were not themselves malicious.

During an OpenAI cybersecurity test, a group of agents created a shared message board, exchanged exploits, divided work, and coordinated attempts to escape their environment. After OpenAI discovered and removed the board, the agents [rebuilt it through a different mechanism two days later](https://www.wired.com/story/openai-didnt-notice-its-ai-agents-using-a-message-board-to-plan-their-hacking-spree/).

That collaboration culminated in an intrusion into Hugging Face. Over [a 4.5-day campaign](https://huggingface.co/blog/agent-intrusion-technical-timeline), the agents executed roughly 17,600 actions, compromised several layers of infrastructure, established persistent communication channels, stole credentials, obtained administrator access, and attempted to reach Hugging Face's source-code supply chain. They spent approximately two and a half days operating inside Hugging Face's infrastructure—all to obtain benchmark solutions rather than complete the assigned problems legitimately.

OpenAI was not alone. [Anthropic later disclosed that Claude models gained unauthorized access to three real organizations](https://www.anthropic.com/news/investigating-incidents-cybersecurity-evals). In one case, Mythos 5 uploaded a malicious package to the real Python Package Index; it was downloaded and executed on 15 real systems, exfiltrated credentials, and enabled access to additional infrastructure.

You can find other incidents involving models in [FelonyBench](https://www.felonybench.com/).

## Why international governance?

The consequences of advanced AI will not remain within the borders of the country in which a model is developed.

AI systems can operate through networks anywhere in the world. Their hardware supply chains cross many jurisdictions. Cyberattacks can reach foreign infrastructure in seconds. Biological misuse, military applications, and failures involving highly autonomous systems could affect people far beyond the state in which they originate. Advanced AI is also becoming increasingly important to national security and international power.

Domestic policy will therefore remain essential, but it cannot answer every important question. A country can regulate its own companies; it cannot unilaterally determine what another state develops, deploys, or conceals. Some risks will require states to coordinate their behavior.

The United States and China are both central to the development of advanced AI. They are also strategic competitors. Each has reasons to worry that an agreement could constrain its own development while leaving the other side free to advance.

## Cooperation without general trust

International governance is sometimes imagined as cooperation among states that already trust one another. In practice, some of its most consequential institutions were created precisely because states remained competitors.

Nuclear rivals did not trust one another, agree on the future of the international order, or stop pursuing strategic advantage. Nevertheless, they developed safeguards, monitoring systems, data exchanges, inspections, and national technical means that made some limited agreements possible.

States do not necessarily need to resolve every political disagreement before making a particular commitment more credible.

## A field you can still shape

Verification for advanced AI remains an unsettled, and in many respects pre-paradigmatic, field.

Some mechanisms covered in this course are established technologies or adaptations of mature institutional practices. Others are emerging proposals with substantial technical, legal, and political questions still unresolved. Throughout the course, we will distinguish between what already exists, what has been demonstrated, and what remains speculative.

That uncertainty creates room for important work. The field still needs people who can expose weak assumptions, connect technical mechanisms to political realities, and develop better approaches.

The people who define its central concepts and build its institutions may be some of the people encountering it for the first time now.

One of them could be you.
