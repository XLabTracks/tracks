The world argues endlessly about whether AI poses imminent dangers to humanity. Almost nobody works on the question that actually decides it: if everyone agreed tomorrow to pause its development, how would we know anyone kept their word? It’s straightforward to prove a weapon is being built. It’s near impossible to know the opposite: that no weapon is being covertly developed anywhere. This course will not give you the solution to verification, because there is no clear-cut answer. Rather, it will give you the tools to assess which solutions are better or more feasible than others, and how to scaffold unreliable layers together into a more reliable system.

By the end of the course, you will have gained the necessary knowledge and strategies to generate real, informed impact in the critical but overlooked space of AI verification. We’ll first justify and contextualize the importance and history of verification to effective policymaking in AI safety. Afterwards, we’ll map the competing incentives and relationships of actors relevant to the verification process, including nation-states, frontier model labs, and the chip production supply chain. Then, we’ll dive into options for anti-ASI policies between adversarial nation-states—and the various buckets of feasible verification methodologies for them: hardware, cloud, intelligence, and human. We’ll make sure to analyze each verification mechanism with a cost mindset: what are the failure modes? How could motivated actors circumvent this mechanism? How do we preserve privacy and bulletproof verification layers? Finally, we will explore how to put these different verification mechanisms together into a coherent verification regime using the Swiss Cheese principle. At the end of the course, you will have the knowledge and skillset to sketch out your own pause treaty, map out a reliable and effective verification regime, and assess existing policy for feasibility and effectiveness, given the time, resource, and infrastructure constraints of the AI race.

You should take this course if you are:

- A technical AI safety researcher aiming to translate technical knowledge into politically feasible and effective policy
- A law/policy student or professional motivated to write and advocate for technically grounded policy to regulate the development of dangerous AI capabilities

### Learning objectives:

- Translate a proposed international AI commitment into verifiable claims by specifying the covered actors, activities, thresholds, declarations, evidence requirements, and conditions that would constitute compliance or non-compliance.
- Map relevant actors and verification opportunities across the AI compute supply chain, from semiconductor equipment and fabrication to cloud infrastructure, model training, and deployment.
- Evaluate hardware, cloud, intelligence and human verification mechanisms according to the claims they can test, the evidence they produce, their implementation requirements, and their principal failure modes.
- Distinguish load-bearing verification mechanisms from less effective or feasible ones.
- Analyze plausible evasion strategies and evaluate which combinations of monitoring, corroboration, inspection, and enforcement could detect, deter, or mitigate them.
- Produce clear, actor-aware policy analysis for a specified audience
