## The brief

"We would coordinate on safety, but antitrust" is one of the most-repeated
lines in frontier AI, and one of the least tested. The orphan catalogue's
complaint is procedural rather than legal: the proposal exists, nobody has
drafted the letter, so nobody has ever been told no.

Write the paperwork.

- **The conduct.** Precisely what the developers want to do together: agree a
  minimum evaluation standard, share an incident taxonomy, set a floor on
  pre-deployment testing. One or two things, described the way a lawyer would
  have to describe them — who meets, what is exchanged, what is agreed.
- **The request letter.** Addressed to the division that issues business
  review or advisory opinions, following the format that process actually
  uses. Facts, the competitive analysis, the assurance sought.
- **The competitive analysis.** Why this is not a cartel: what it does not
  cover (price, output, allocation, capability roadmaps), and the safeguards
  that keep it there.
- **The anticipated response.** Write the agency's likely reply, including its
  most awkward question. Then say what conduct your letter has cleared and
  what it explicitly has not.

## Why it exists

Most policy learners write documents addressed to nobody. This one has a real
recipient, a real format, and a real process — and the exercise of writing to
a form you did not design is most of the education. Governance work is
substantially the craft of getting an existing process to do something.

It also usefully deflates a talking point. Once you have written the request,
you can say concretely how much of the "antitrust problem" is legal exposure
and how much is a reason not to have the conversation.

## Scope

**In scope:** published agency guidance and past business review letters in
other industries, the standard-setting antitrust literature, and public
statements by developers about why they do not coordinate.

**Out of scope:** actually submitting it, and jurisdictions beyond the one you
pick. If you want the EU angle, that is a second memo.

**You are not required to conclude that the waiver is a good idea.** A strong
submission may end with "the letter is easy to write and would clear less than
people think" — that is a finding, and it is better evidenced than the talking
point it replaces.

## What good looks like

| Dimension | Weak | Strong |
|---|---|---|
| The conduct | "Discuss safety standards" | One or two specified activities, with what is exchanged and what is not |
| Format | A policy memo | Follows the agency's actual request format, with its required elements |
| Analysis | Asserts pro-competitive intent | Names the conduct that would still be unlawful and how the safeguards prevent it |
| Honesty | Concludes the problem is solved | States what remains uncleared, and whether that was the real blocker |

## Getting started

1. Read two real business review letters from any industry before drafting.
   The format teaches you what the agency wants to be told.
2. Narrow the conduct until it is boring. Ambitious coordination is what makes
   the request unanswerable.
3. Write the agency's hardest question in week one and let it shape the rest.
