## The brief

Pick one published frontier safety framework. Find the commitment that
matters most and the commitment that is vaguest — often the same one — and
do two things.

**First, the critique.** Clause by clause, for the section you chose:

- What triggers the obligation, and who decides that it triggered?
- What evidence would establish the trigger, and who produces it?
- What happens on trigger — a pause, a mitigation, a disclosure — and by
  when?
- Where does the framework grade its own homework?

**Second, the redraft.** One commitment, rewritten so that the trigger is
measurable with evals that exist, the decision rule is legible from
outside, and the escape hatches are named rather than left implicit. Then
state honestly what your redraft costs: over-triggering, gaming, or a bar
so specific that the next capability jump routes around it.

## Why it exists

If-then commitments are the main instrument labs have offered in place of
regulation, and their weakest property is that the party measuring the
trigger is the party bound by it. Learning to read one at clause level —
and to write one that would survive an adversarial reading — is directly
transferable to the regulatory drafting that will eventually copy this
language.

## Scope

**In scope:** one framework, one commitment, public eval literature.

**Out of scope:** a comparative survey of every lab's framework. Depth
beats coverage; a table of five frameworks with one line each teaches
nothing you did not already know.

## What good looks like

- The critique quotes the actual language and reasons about it, rather than
  summarising the framework's intent.
- Your redraft points at evals that exist, and you name them.
- You state the failure mode of your own version. Every sharpening trades
  robustness for something.

## Getting started

1. Print the section. Mark every modal verb — *may*, *will*, *should*,
   *intends to*. The gradient of commitment is visible in the verbs.
2. Ask, of each trigger: could an outsider tell whether this fired? If not,
   that is your clause.
3. Write your redraft before reading anyone else's critique of the same
   framework.
