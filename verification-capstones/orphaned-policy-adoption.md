## The brief

Some governance proposals are not contested — they are simply unowned. No
agency has the mandate, no advocacy organisation has it on the list, no
lab benefits from proposing it. Pick one and build its adoption case.

Deliver:

- **The orphan.** The proposal, stated in a paragraph, with its origin and
  its best public articulation.
- **The autopsy.** Why it has no owner. Distinguish "no one benefits" from
  "the beneficiaries are diffuse" from "the natural owner lacks the
  authority" — the three have completely different fixes.
- **The stakeholder map.** Who would have to act, in what order, with what
  incentives, and who is positioned to block.
- **The implementation plan.** A sequence, with the first move specified
  well enough that someone could make it next quarter.
- **The scenario test.** Run the plan against short and long timeline
  worlds. Say which one it bets on, or make it robust to both.

## Why it exists

Most policy training teaches you to evaluate proposals. Almost none
teaches you what has to be true for a proposal to acquire an owner — which
is the step where good ideas actually die. This capstone puts the learner
on the institutional side of the problem, where the deliverable is a
sequence of moves rather than an argument.

## Scope

**In scope:** proposals with a public articulation you can cite. Curated
lists of unowned policy questions and project-idea collections are the
intended starting point.

**Out of scope:** inventing a new proposal. The exercise is adoption, not
ideation — and an invented proposal cannot be checked for why it stalled.

## What good looks like

- The autopsy has a mechanism, not a mood. "It's unglamorous" is a
  hypothesis; "the only body with rulemaking authority here has no
  statutory hook and the fix requires primary legislation" is an autopsy.
- The first move is small, concrete, and does not require anyone to already
  agree with you.
- The scenario test changes something. A plan identical in both timeline
  worlds has usually not been tested.

## Getting started

1. Shortlist three orphans, then pick the one where you can name a
   plausible owner. An orphan with no candidate parent is a research
   question, not a capstone.
2. Interview-shaped reading: find what the natural owner has actually said
   about the area, not what you assume they think.
3. Write the first move before the full sequence. If the first move is
   "convene a working group", keep going.
