## The brief

There are, publicly and for free, several hundred AI governance research
ideas that nobody has picked up. They are ideas, not projects. Turn one into a
project.

Two pages, in the shape a [request for
proposals](https://coefficientgiving.org/funds/navigating-transformative-ai/request-for-proposals-improving-capability-evaluations/)
asks for:

- **The question**, in one sentence, answerable in the time you are asking
  for.
- **Why it is not answered already.** The three closest existing pieces of
  work and what each leaves open. This section is where most proposals are
  actually rejected.
- **Method.** What you would do, in enough detail that a reader can imagine
  the working week. Sources, interviews, datasets, analysis.
- **Output and audience.** What exists at the end, and who reads it. "A
  report" is not an audience.
- **Budget and timeline.** Real numbers. Your own time at a defensible rate,
  plus anything you have to buy. Milestones with dates.
- **The falsifier.** What result would tell you the project failed, and what
  you would do then. Proposals without this read as advocacy.
- **Risks.** Including the honest one: what makes this project not worth
  funding.

## Why it exists

This is the cheapest capstone in the bank and one of the most transferable.
Everything downstream of the program — grants, fellowship applications, a
first job in a policy shop — is gated on a two-page document with this exact
shape. Most people write their first one under deadline, badly.

It also teaches a specific discipline the track cares about: the difference
between a topic and a question. An idea list gives you topics. A funder buys
questions, with a stated end.

## Scope

**In scope:** any idea from a published agenda, or one of your own if you can
point at the gap it fills. Desk research to establish what exists.

**Out of scope:** doing the research. This is the proposal, and the temptation
to start answering the question is the main way this capstone overruns.

**Two pages is the constraint, not a target.** A four-page proposal that
"needed the space" has failed the exercise. Cutting is the skill.

## What good looks like

| Dimension | Weak | Strong |
|---|---|---|
| The question | "Investigate compute governance" | One sentence with a scope, a method and an end date implied in it |
| Prior work | "Little has been written" | Three named pieces and exactly what each leaves open |
| Budget | Omitted or round | Itemised, with your rate stated and the largest line defended |
| Falsifier | Absent | A specific result that would end the project, and what you would do next |

The tell of a strong proposal: a reader who disagrees with your conclusion
can still tell you exactly which line of the budget to cut.

## Getting started

1. Read thirty ideas before choosing one. The first idea that excites you is
   usually the one that is exciting because it is vague.
2. Do the prior-work search before writing anything else. It kills roughly
   half of all candidate questions, and killing one in week one is free.
3. Write the falsifier third, not last. If you cannot state one, the question
   is not yet a question.
