## The brief

Policy windows open without warning and close fast. When one opens, whoever has
a costed plan on the shelf wins, and everyone else writes one in a week and
spends it badly. There is no shelf-ready AI resilience plan at scale.

Write one.

- **The allocation.** Line items with amounts. Alignment and safety research,
  evaluation and audit capacity, security for model weights, government
  technical capacity, biosecurity and cyber defence hardening, compute for
  independent researchers, workforce and institutional capacity. Your split,
  your reasoning.
- **Absorptive capacity.** The constraint that makes this hard and that most
  wish-lists ignore. For each line: could the money be spent well *next year*,
  or does the field lack the people to absorb it? A line that needs five years
  of pipeline-building to use is a different instrument from one that scales
  tomorrow, and mixing them is how large programmes waste money visibly.
- **Sequencing.** What happens in year one, two, three. Which lines unlock
  others.
- **The theory of change per line.** One sentence each: what changes because
  this was funded. Lines that cannot pass this test come out.
- **The cut list.** Ranked. What goes first if the number becomes two billion,
  and what you would protect down to the last dollar. This section is the one a
  staffer will actually read.
- **Scenario robustness.** Run the plan across short and long timelines. Say
  which lines are bets on one scenario and which hold in both.

## Why it exists

Week 8 teaches that timing decides which recommendations land, and that
strategic premises silently drive policy disagreements. A budget makes both
visible: you cannot allocate ten billion dollars without revealing your
timeline beliefs, and the argument you have with your teammates about the split
*is* the strategic disagreement, made numerate.

Absorptive capacity is the specific lesson worth the four weeks. The instinct is
to fund the most important thing; the constraint is what the field can actually
convert into work this year. Learning to separate those two changes how you read
every funding announcement afterwards.

## Scope

**In scope:** public information on current funding levels, organisation sizes,
and existing government programmes as cost anchors. Order-of-magnitude figures.

**Out of scope:** the politics of getting the money appropriated, and precision.
This is an allocation argument, not an appropriations strategy.

**Ten billion is the constraint, not a wish list.** If your lines sum to more,
you have not done the exercise; the cutting is the work.

## What good looks like

| Dimension | Weak | Strong |
|---|---|---|
| Lines | Categories with amounts | Amounts anchored to real current spend and real organisation sizes |
| Absorptive capacity | Ignored | Per line, whether it could be spent well next year, with the bottleneck named |
| Theory of change | "Advances safety" | One sentence per line saying what changes, and lines cut for failing it |
| Cut list | Absent | Ranked, down to a two-billion version, with the protected core identified |

The strongest plans allocate visibly less than they would like to the line the
team cares most about, because that field cannot absorb it yet — and say so.

## Getting started

1. Find the current annual spend on two of your lines in week one. Without
   anchors every number is arbitrary and the plan reads as fantasy.
2. Do the cut list before finishing the full plan. It reveals what you actually
   believe, and the full plan then has to justify itself against it.
3. Have each team member write the split independently before comparing. Where
   you diverge is the strategic disagreement, and it is worth a session.
