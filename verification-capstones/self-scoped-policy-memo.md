## The brief

Two pages. One question, scoped by you. One audience, named. One decision,
specified. At least one artifact from earlier in the track carrying the
evidentiary weight.

The non-negotiable fields, pinned at the top before the prose:

- **Audience.** A role that exists, at an institution that exists, with
  the authority to do the thing you are recommending.
- **Decision.** The specific choice in front of them — not "consider
  prioritising", but the fork.
- **Falsifier.** What would show you wrong. Mandatory, in the memo, in the
  reader's eyeline.

This is the track's certificate assessment for async learners, and it is
public by default.

## Why it exists

The scaffolding fades on purpose. Weeks 1–3 hand over templates and worked
examples; the middle weeks loosen; the capstone has no template. Choosing
the question *is* the assessment — a well-executed memo on a question
nobody needed answered is the failure mode this exists to expose.

It also does double duty as an application artifact. Readers cannot verify
invisible work: "researched AI policy and organised discussions" loses to
a linkable memo every time.

## Scope

**In scope:** any governance question where you can name a real audience
and a real decision, and where an artifact you already built supplies at
least part of the evidence.

**Out of scope:** two pages means two pages. An appendix is allowed; a
six-page memo with a two-page summary is not the genre and will be graded
as if the first two pages were the whole submission.

## What good looks like

- It survives the skim test. First sentences plus your bold lines, read
  alone, deliver the recommendation and its main support.
- The path to impact has a mechanism. "AI will be important" with no
  causal chain is the single most common rejection reason in adjacent
  fellowship pipelines.
- The falsifier is real. "Unless the evidence changes" is not a falsifier;
  "if the 2027 procurement data shows uptake below X, this recommendation
  is wrong" is.
- The evidence base is *yours*. A memo that cites only other people's
  analysis has skipped the part the track was for.

## Getting started

1. Write the audience and decision lines before the question. Half of all
   scoping problems dissolve once a named person is in the room.
2. Pick the artifact you will lean on, and check it actually supports the
   claim you want to make. It often supports a narrower one — take the
   narrower one.
3. Draft the falsifier third. If you cannot write one, the recommendation
   is not yet specific enough to be useful.
