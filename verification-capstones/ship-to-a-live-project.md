## The brief

Every other capstone in this bank is graded by the program. This one is
graded by a stranger who did not agree to teach you.

The sequence:

1. **Find a project that is asking.** The [aisafety.com projects
   directory](https://www.aisafety.com/projects) lists initiatives seeking
   volunteers. Pick one whose work you can already read.
2. **Agree the scope in writing.** One message to the maintainer proposing a
   specific contribution, with what you will deliver and by when. Get a yes
   before you build. This step is the capstone; the rest is execution.
3. **Ship it.** A documentation fix, a dataset addition, a test suite, a
   translated resource, a small feature, a research summary — whatever they
   said yes to.
4. **Take the review.** Revise until it lands or the maintainer closes it.
5. **Write the note.** Two pages: how you scoped it, what the review changed,
   what the maintainer cared about that you did not expect, and what you would
   propose next.

## Why it exists

Program work has a soft edge: the audience is hypothetical, the deadline is
internal, and a mentor is invested in your success. Real contributions have
none of that. A maintainer with no time will tell you plainly that your scope
was too big, your patch touched too much, or your summary missed the point —
and that feedback is worth more than a graded memo.

It is also the shortest path from "did a program" to "has done something in
the field", which is the distinction that gets read on an application.

## Scope

**In scope:** one contribution, agreed in advance, small enough that a busy
maintainer can review it in under an hour. Small and merged beats ambitious
and abandoned.

**Out of scope:** unsolicited large contributions, and anything you start
before the maintainer has said yes. Also out of scope: counting your own
program work as the contribution.

**This capstone can fail for reasons that are not your fault.** Maintainers go
quiet. Projects stall. That risk is why it is marked *concept* rather than
*ready*, and why the mitigation is structural: contact **three** projects in
week one, not one. If nobody responds by the end of week two, the note becomes
the deliverable on its own — what you proposed, to whom, and what the silence
suggests about how the field onboards volunteers. That is a legitimate and
genuinely useful outcome, and it should be graded as one.

## What good looks like

| Dimension | Weak | Strong |
|---|---|---|
| Scoping | "Asked how I could help" | A specific proposal with a deliverable and a date, agreed before work started |
| Size | A large patch touching many things | One thing, reviewable in under an hour |
| Review | Defended the original approach | Revised, and can say what the maintainer's standard was |
| The note | A diary | A transferable account of how this project decides what it wants |

## Getting started

1. Contact three projects on day one, with three different specific proposals.
   Generic offers to help are the most-ignored message maintainers receive.
2. Read their contribution guide and their last ten merged changes before
   proposing anything. Proposing something they already rejected is the
   fastest way to be ignored.
3. Halve your scope before you send it. Then halve it again if the project has
   fewer than five active contributors.
