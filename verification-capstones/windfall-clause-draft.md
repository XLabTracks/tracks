## The brief

The proposal is six years old. It has tax brackets, a rationale, and a
conference paper. What it has never had is a paragraph a lawyer could put in
front of a board. The orphan catalogue is blunt about it: nobody has drafted a
sample clause, and nobody has persuaded a corporation to adopt one.

Produce:

- **The clause.** Actual operative language. Trigger, measurement, obligation,
  recipient, governance of the recipient, amendment and termination terms.
- **The corporate-form memo.** Pick one real developer and read its actual
  structure — charter, capped-profit arrangement, trust, PBC status, whatever
  it is. Say where your clause attaches, who has authority to adopt it, and
  what it would take: a board resolution, a charter amendment, a shareholder
  vote.
- **The trigger problem.** "Windfall" has to be a number attached to something
  measurable. Revenue? Profit as a share of global product? Say what you chose
  and what it does under transfer pricing, a holding company, or a spin-out.
- **Three ways out.** How a determined company escapes your clause, and the
  language that closes each — or the admission that it cannot be closed.

## Why it exists

Week 3 teaches you to read an RSP and find every weasel word and exit hatch.
This asks you to write a commitment that will be read that way by someone
hostile, and to price every word you soften to get it signed.

It is also the clearest case in the catalogue of a proposal that stalled at
exactly the drafting step. That is worth noticing: the gap between "a good idea
with a paper behind it" and "a document someone could sign" is where a great
deal of governance work actually dies.

## Scope

**In scope:** one company, its public corporate documents, the original
proposal, and comparable clauses from other industries (charitable pledges,
profit-sharing, contingent value rights).

**Out of scope:** the philosophical case for redistribution. The proposal made
it; you are past that point. Also out of scope: tax treatment across
jurisdictions — flag it as an open question in one paragraph and move on.

## What good looks like

| Dimension | Weak | Strong |
|---|---|---|
| The clause | A restated principle | Operative language with a defined trigger and a named obligation |
| Corporate form | Generic | Read against one company's real charter, with the adoption route named |
| Escape routes | "Enforcement would be difficult" | Three specific manoeuvres, each with the language that closes it or an admission |
| Adoptability | Maximally strong | A version you think a board might actually pass, and what it cost to get there |

## Getting started

1. Read the company's charter before you read the proposal again. The
   proposal is about design; the charter tells you what can attach to it.
2. Write the trigger first. Everything else is easy by comparison, and a
   clause with a vague trigger is the thing that has already failed once.
3. Draft the escape routes before polishing. They rewrite the clause.
